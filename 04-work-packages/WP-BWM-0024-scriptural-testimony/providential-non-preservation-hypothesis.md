# Theological Research Note — Providential Non-Preservation of the Autographs

Status: captured hypothesis
Parent: WP-BWM-0024 — Scripture as a Trustworthy Explanatory Source
Date: 2026-09-21

## Thesis

God providentially preserved the truth of His written Word through a distributed textual tradition while permitting the physical autographs to disappear.

A possible providential purpose is that preservation of unique original artifacts would have created objects susceptible to veneration or idolatry, a recurrent human tendency explicitly demonstrated in Scripture.

This is a **theological explanatory hypothesis**, not a claim established directly by manuscript evidence or by an explicit biblical statement concerning the fate of the autographs.

## Working name

**Providential Non-Preservation Hypothesis (PNPH)**

## Core distinction

```text
Preservation of revelation
        ≠
Preservation of the revelatory artifact
```

The authority resides in the truth God communicates. It does not require continuing possession of the physical sheet, scroll, tablet, or codex on which an inspired author first wrote.

## Biblical pattern

### 1. The bronze serpent

Numbers 21:8–9 records that God commanded Moses to make the bronze serpent for a legitimate divinely ordained purpose.

Second Kings 18:4 records that Hezekiah later destroyed that same object because Israel had begun making offerings to it.

This establishes a relevant anthropological and theological pattern:

```text
authentic sacred artifact
        +
genuine history of divine use
        ↓
object of misplaced religious devotion
```

The object's authentic association with divine action did not require its perpetual preservation once it became an object of improper devotion.

### 2. Moses' burial place

Deuteronomy 34:5–6 states that Moses died in Moab and that his burial location was unknown.

This does not establish why God concealed or permitted loss of the location. It does demonstrate that preservation of access to the physical remains of the covenant mediator was unnecessary to preservation of the revelation and covenant history associated with him.

### 3. General artifact/revelation distinction

Scripture's authority repeatedly survives independently of continued possession of the physical artifacts associated with revelatory events.

This pattern should be investigated further rather than asserted broadly without textual support.

Candidate cases for study:
- bronze serpent;
- Moses' grave;
- stone tablets / ark traditions and later absence;
- temple objects and their loss/destruction;
- prophetic scrolls that were destroyed and rewritten, especially Jeremiah 36.

Jeremiah 36 may be especially important because destruction of a physical prophetic manuscript does not destroy the divine message: the scroll is rewritten, with additional words. This potentially provides a direct biblical instance of **message continuity without artifact continuity**.

## Relationship to textual transmission

The manuscript evidence currently being developed in Tranche B shows preservation through multiplicity, copying, geographic distribution, comparison, and recoverability rather than through custody of a single surviving autograph.

PNPH asks whether that historical state is providentially fitting:

```text
No surviving autograph
        +
distributed manuscript tradition
        +
recoverable textual content
        ↓
authority remains attached to the Word
rather than a unique physical relic
```

This does not mean that textual variants were divinely caused for the purpose of preventing idolatry. That stronger proposition is neither required nor presently warranted.

## Epistemic status

### Established from Scripture

**HIGH**
- Humans can convert an authentic object associated with God's action into an object of improper worship: Numbers 21:8–9 → 2 Kings 18:4.
- Scripture records at least one highly significant sacred person's burial place as unavailable: Deuteronomy 34:6.
- Biblical revelation does not make continuing possession of those physical artifacts necessary for covenantal or doctrinal truth.

### Historically observable

**HIGH**
- No biblical autograph is presently known to survive.
- Biblical texts are instead witnessed through manuscript traditions.

These historical propositions require continued manuscript/source documentation in Tranche B.

### Theological inference

**MEDIUM / HYPOTHESIS**
- God purposefully permitted the autographs to disappear in part to prevent their becoming objects of idolatrous veneration.

The inference is coherent with the biblical anthropology of idolatry and the artifact/revelation distinction, but Scripture does not presently appear to state this purpose explicitly.

## No-smuggling rule

PNPH must not be used circularly:

```text
Autographs are absent
→ therefore God intended their absence
→ therefore their absence proves providential preservation.
```

That inference is invalid.

Instead:

```text
Scripture independently establishes human artifact-idolatry
+
Scripture distinguishes divine truth from physical artifact continuity
+
history presents a distributed textual tradition without surviving autographs
        ↓
PNPH becomes a coherent theological explanation of that state of affairs
```

The hypothesis remains subordinate to the Scriptural Testimony Principle and is not required for STP to succeed.

## Potential explanatory value

If sustained, PNPH may explain why the actual preservation architecture is theologically fitting:

1. **No unique relic controls the text.**
2. **Distributed witnesses permit comparison and correction.**
3. **Loss of one manuscript cannot extinguish the revelation.**
4. **Authority attaches to the communicated Word rather than material substrate.**
5. **Textual confidence can arise from convergent witnesses rather than custody claims surrounding a sacred object.**

The final point may be especially useful to BWM: distributed preservation provides an epistemically inspectable tradition while avoiding dependence upon the provenance of one allegedly sacred autograph.

## Research questions

1. Does Scripture provide additional examples of divinely significant artifacts being lost, destroyed, replaced, or made inaccessible?
2. Does Jeremiah 36 provide a canonical model of revelation surviving destruction of a physical manuscript?
3. How did Jewish and Christian communities historically treat purported biblical autographs or apostolic relics?
4. Is there evidence that manuscripts associated with apostles or saints became objects of veneration?
5. Have theologians previously proposed providential reasons for the non-preservation of the autographs?
6. How does classical Protestant bibliology distinguish inspiration of the autographs from providential preservation of the text?
7. Can PNPH be stated without implying that God positively wills scribal error?
8. Does a distributed witness architecture provide identifiable epistemic advantages over a single-artifact preservation model?
