# WP-BWM-0024 — Canon Chronology and Recognition Ledger

Status: active research
Date opened: 2026-09-21

## Purpose

Document how the biblical canon was composed, circulated, used, recognized, collected, and eventually listed, using primary sources wherever possible.

The governing distinction is:

```text
Composition → Circulation → Use → Recognition → Collection → Canon Lists
```

These stages overlap historically. They must not be collapsed into the popular claim that a late church council “chose the books of the Bible.”

## Methodological guardrails

1. A manuscript collection is evidence of books copied together, not automatically a formal canon list.
2. A church father's citation of a book is evidence of use, not automatically evidence that the author regarded it as canonical Scripture.
3. A disputed book may be widely read without being universally recognized.
4. A canon list records a judgment already situated in a history of use and recognition; it does not necessarily create the authority of the listed books.
5. Regional disagreement must remain visible.
6. Old Testament and New Testament canon histories must be treated separately before synthesis.

## NT-CAN-001 — Fourfold Gospel and Pauline corpus before fourth-century lists

By the time of Eusebius, the four Gospels, Acts, Pauline letters, 1 John, and 1 Peter belong to his acknowledged category, indicating that recognition of a substantial New Testament core precedes the later exact 27-book list.

**Relation:** primary patristic evidence for prior recognition.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

Primary anchor:
- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25.

## NT-CAN-002 — Eusebius records acknowledged and disputed books

Eusebius does not portray the New Testament as a collection invented by his generation. He distinguishes acknowledged writings from books that are disputed yet recognized by many.

His disputed category includes James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2–3 John. Revelation receives mixed treatment because Eusebius records differing judgments.

**Relation:** primary evidence for both broad agreement and residual fourth-century dispute.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

**STP significance:** canon history contains real uncertainty at the edges while showing an already recognized core.

## NT-CAN-003 — Eusebius distinguishes disputed Christian works from other writings

Eusebius also names works such as the Shepherd, Apocalypse of Peter, Barnabas, and Didache in categories outside his acknowledged core, while distinguishing these from writings he associates with heretical apostolic claims.

**Relation:** primary evidence that early Christian literature was being differentiated rather than treated as one undifferentiated pool.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

**Qualification:** Eusebius's terminology and categories require careful translation and contextual analysis. Modern labels such as “canonical,” “spurious,” and “rejected” can oversimplify his Greek categories.

## NT-CAN-004 — Hebrews demonstrates regional/authorship complexity

Eusebius reports that Paul's letters were broadly known but also records that some in the Roman church disputed Hebrews on Pauline-authorship grounds.

**Relation:** primary evidence for regional/authorship disagreement concerning a book ultimately retained in the canon.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

**STP significance:** canonical reception need not imply universal agreement about authorship.

## NT-CAN-005 — Athanasius 39 provides the exact 27-book NT list

Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter, AD 367, lists the four Gospels, Acts, seven Catholic Epistles, fourteen Pauline letters including Hebrews, and Revelation, yielding the same 27 New Testament books used today.

**Relation:** primary canonical-list evidence.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

Primary anchor:
- Athanasius, Festal Letter 39.5–6.

## NT-CAN-006 — Athanasius distinguishes canonical books from books useful for reading

Athanasius does not divide literature simply into “Bible” and “worthless.” After his canonical list he names books appointed for reading/instruction, including Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the Didache, and Shepherd.

**Relation:** primary evidence for graded reception/use categories.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

**STP significance:** ecclesial usefulness and canonical status were distinguishable categories.

## NT-CAN-007 — Athanasius frames the list as received, not newly invented

Athanasius describes the canonical books as handed down and accredited, presenting his list as a clarification of received ecclesial tradition in response to competing writings.

**Relation:** primary evidence concerning Athanasius's own understanding of canonical authority.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

**Qualification:** his self-description is evidence for how he understood the process; it does not by itself prove universal agreement across all fourth-century churches.

## NT-CAN-008 — Exact-list agreement occurs after a longer recognition process

The contrast between Eusebius's acknowledged/disputed categories in the early fourth century and Athanasius's exact 27-book list in 367 shows consolidation at the edges rather than creation ex nihilo of an entire New Testament collection.

**Relation:** historical synthesis from primary patristic evidence.  
**Confidence:** HIGH.

**Qualification:** additional witnesses between and around these authors must be added before claiming a single linear development across all regions.

## Working chronology

### Stage 1 — Composition

First-century Christian writings are composed in particular historical communities and circumstances.

### Stage 2 — Circulation

Individual writings and collections circulate beyond their initial recipients. Manuscript evidence such as early Pauline codices bears directly on this stage.

### Stage 3 — Use

Churches and writers read, cite, teach from, and copy particular works. Use can precede explicit canonical terminology.

### Stage 4 — Recognition

Writings are treated as authoritative or scriptural within communities and across regions. Recognition is uneven at the edges.

### Stage 5 — Collection

Gospels, Pauline letters, Catholic Epistles, and eventually broader codices are physically and conceptually collected.

### Stage 6 — Canon lists

Writers and synods produce explicit lists that make boundaries visible. These lists are historical evidence for reception and boundary definition.

## Claims audit

### Claim: “The Council of Nicaea chose the books of the Bible.”

**Status:** REJECT pending any primary evidence.

The initial primary-source canon evidence being assembled here does not support this claim. The BWM public case should not repeat it.

### Claim: “Athanasius created the 27-book New Testament in 367.”

**Status:** REJECT.

Athanasius supplies the earliest surviving exact 27-book list currently identified in this research baseline, but Eusebius already documents an acknowledged core and disputed edge before Athanasius. Athanasius himself presents the books as handed down and accredited.

### Claim: “The canon was obvious and universally agreed from the beginning.”

**Status:** REJECT.

Eusebius explicitly documents disputes over James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Revelation, and the reception/authorship of Hebrews.

### Claim: “Because some books were disputed, there was no meaningful canon before the fourth century.”

**Status:** REJECT.

Eusebius's categories themselves demonstrate the opposite structure: a substantial acknowledged collection alongside a smaller disputed edge.

## Provisional public formulation

> **The New Testament canon was not created in a single late vote. The historical evidence shows an earlier core of widely acknowledged writings, continuing discussion over a smaller group of books, and later lists that make the boundaries increasingly explicit. The process involved circulation, use, recognition, collection, and clarification over time.**

This remains provisional until the earlier second- and third-century witnesses and conciliar evidence are added.
