# WP-BWM-0024 — Scripture as a Trustworthy Explanatory Source

Status: proposed

## Purpose

Formalize a BWM hard-core commitment concerning Scripture as a trustworthy explanatory source for reality and develop the positive cumulative case that supports rational confidence in that commitment.

This package is constructive rather than a skeptical deconstruction of Scripture. Its purpose is to explain why confidence in biblical testimony is epistemically warranted across domains such as history, wisdom, human behavior, moral order, natural order, and origins, and why Scripture is therefore admissible as testimony concerning aspects of reality and history that are not directly available to present human observation.

## Hard-core commitment

> **Scripture is truthful divine revelation and therefore provides reliable testimony concerning reality wherever it speaks, including matters of history, human nature, moral order, creation, and divine action. Because the same God is the author of Scripture and Creator of the natural world, genuine revelation and genuine observation cannot ultimately conflict. Apparent conflicts call for examination of our interpretation of Scripture, our interpretation of nature, or both.**

Working name: **Scriptural Testimony Principle (STP).**

The STP belongs in the BWM hard core. Particular scientific, chronological, geological, biological, or cosmological models remain corrigible components of the protective belt.

## Research question

Why is it rational to treat Scripture as a trustworthy explanatory source when reconstructing reality and its history, including domains that exceed the explanatory scope of methodological naturalism?

The argument should establish a cumulative positive case:

```text
Scriptural authority
        +
demonstrated explanatory coherence
        +
correspondence with accessible reality
        ↓
rational confidence in Scripture as testimony about reality
        +
Scriptural testimony concerning origins
        ↓
Genesis is legitimate evidence in historical reconstruction
```

This is a warrant argument, not a claim that corroboration in one domain deductively proves every biblical proposition.

## Scope

### 1. Historical testimony

Assess biblical claims that intersect independently accessible history:

- persons and peoples
- geography and places
- political institutions
- cultural practices
- conflicts and events
- chronological and contextual claims

Maintain the distinction between corroboration of historical context and corroboration of a particular event.

### 2. Human nature and behavior

Develop the biblical explanatory account of recurrent human behavior, including:

- self-interest and ordered self-love
- pride, status, and power
- tribalism and partiality
- rationalization and self-deception
- desire and temptation
- envy and rivalry
- guilt and conscience
- violence and reconciliation
- family and social obligation
- cooperation, sacrifice, and love
- knowledge of moral norms alongside violation of them

Compare these claims with ordinary experience and relevant work in history, psychology, behavioral economics, anthropology, and related fields without making those disciplines epistemically prior to Scripture.

### 3. Wisdom and lived reality

Examine the explanatory and predictive reach of biblical wisdom concerning:

- speech
- work
- wealth
- relationships
- justice
- suffering
- mortality
- prudence
- folly
- meaning

Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Job, Psalms, and the teaching of Christ are primary biblical loci.

### 4. Natural order and intelligibility

Develop the biblical account of creation as:

- real
- ordered
- intelligible
- contingent
- lawlike
- dependent upon God
- accessible to rational investigation
- interpreted by finite and fallible human observers

Clarify why this ontology supports empirical investigation without reducing reality to empirically tractable natural causes.

### 5. Naturalism and explanatory scope

Maintain the categorical distinction between methodological naturalism and metaphysical naturalism.

Methodological constraint:

```text
admissible scientific causes ⊆ empirically tractable natural causes
```

Metaphysical claim:

```text
reality = nature alone
```

The success of the first does not by itself establish the second. The package should show why methodological usefulness cannot be silently converted into an exhaustive ontology.

### 6. Origins and inaccessible history

Apply the cumulative warrant argument to origins.

Present observations are accessible. Initial conditions and unobserved history are reconstructed through models. Genesis claims to provide testimony concerning the beginning from a source not limited to present human observation.

The resulting BWM principle is:

> **If Genesis provides genuine information about initial conditions and early history, its testimony belongs among the constraints against which reconstructions of the observable world are evaluated.**

## Epistemic discipline

The package must preserve two distinctions:

```text
Scripture ≠ our interpretation of Scripture
Nature ≠ our interpretation of Nature
```

Scripture occupies the governing position in the BWM epistemic hierarchy. Human interpretation of Scripture remains corrigible. Scientific observations are real data about God's world. Scientific models and BWM models remain corrigible.

Confidence in Scripture must not be made dependent upon the success of DFM, PFH, CHFM, a particular Flood mechanism, a radiometric proposal, a cosmological mechanism, or any other protective-belt hypothesis.

## Positive posture

This work package is intended to promote warranted confidence.

It should:

- begin from the BWM hard-core commitment to biblical revelation;
- articulate positive reasons for trusting Scripture;
- use corroboration and explanatory success as cumulative support rather than as authorities over Scripture;
- engage serious objections where necessary to clarify the positive case;
- avoid framing Scripture as a defendant presumed unreliable until independently authenticated;
- avoid overstating what individual lines of evidence establish;
- distinguish confidence in Scripture from confidence in any BWM scientific model.

## Public-facing bridge

Develop a concise reader-facing argument along these lines:

> **Why trust the Bible about the beginning?**
>
> We trust sources partly because of how reliably they describe what we can examine for ourselves. The Bible speaks about history, human nature, morality, wisdom, suffering, society, and the world we inhabit. Across those domains it presents a coherent account of reality.
>
> Genesis also speaks about something we cannot examine in the same way: the beginning itself.
>
> If Scripture is trustworthy concerning the reality we can investigate, it is reasonable to take seriously its testimony concerning the history we cannot directly observe.
>
> That is where the Biblical WorldModel begins.

This copy is provisional and must be refined against the evidence developed by the package.

## Source discipline

- Scripture is primary. Use ESV for quoted biblical text.
- Search for and cite primary historical, archaeological, scientific, and scholarly sources before relying on secondary treatments.
- Never represent contextual corroboration as direct verification of an event.
- Where only secondary evidence is available, state that explicitly.
- Where sources conflict, record the divergence rather than silently harmonizing it.
- Maintain confidence markers where useful: HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW, UNCERTAIN.
- Do not fabricate citations or infer support beyond what a source establishes.

## Non-goals

This package does not:

- attempt a skeptical deconstruction of biblical authority;
- require every biblical claim to be independently verified before it can be trusted;
- make archaeology, science, psychology, or another external discipline the final judge of Scripture;
- claim that successful corroboration in one domain deductively proves unrelated claims;
- canonize a particular BWM scientific hypothesis;
- treat metaphysical naturalism as a finding of empirical science;
- conceal open questions or contrary evidence.
