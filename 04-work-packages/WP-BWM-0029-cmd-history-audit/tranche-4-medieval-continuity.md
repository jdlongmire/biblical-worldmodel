# Tranche 4: Medieval Natural Philosophy and Continuity

Status: research note, 2026-09-26

## Finding

The medieval continuity link is supported strongly enough to reject the idea that disciplined natural-causal inquiry appeared only after Christian theology was displaced. The evidence supports a more precise continuity thesis: medieval Latin Christendom institutionalized natural philosophy, developed extensive causal analysis, and maintained theological doctrines under which created causes possessed genuine explanatory roles.

## Aquinas and secondary causation

Aquinas is important because divine providence and creaturely causation are not competitors in his system. Divine governance works through an ordered creation in which created things genuinely act as causes. God's primary causality therefore does not erase secondary causality.

This provides a theological architecture under which investigation of created causes is legitimate rather than impious. It is not identical to seventeenth-century experimental science, and BWM shall not describe it as such.

Influence dimensions:
- ontology of created causation;
- expectation of ordered creation;
- legitimacy of causal investigation;
- distinction between primary and secondary causation.

Confidence: HIGH for Aquinas's framework; MEDIUM for claims about downstream causal influence on later science unless separately demonstrated.

## Medieval causal inquiry

Modern specialist reference scholarship confirms that causality occupied a central place in medieval academic natural philosophy. Medieval authors extensively commented on Aristotle's Physics and other natural works, but causal investigation was not merely passive repetition of Aristotle. Questions concerning efficient and final causes, divine ordered power, natural necessity, and alternative physical possibilities became analytical tools.

The Stanford Encyclopedia's survey notes that rational investigation of the created universe was already connected with theological intelligibility before the full Aristotelian corpus became available. It also documents later medieval use of divine ordered power alongside robust natural causal assertions.

## Institutional continuity

Edward Grant's specialist historical work provides the strongest present secondary support for the institutional continuity thesis.

Grant argues that:
- natural philosophy became deeply embedded in the medieval university;
- it was among the most widely taught disciplines;
- Christian religious authorities came to accept and defend its study;
- medieval natural philosophers regularly questioned Aristotle and developed alternative physical interpretations;
- the period 1200-1500 laid important intellectual and institutional foundations for the seventeenth-century Scientific Revolution.

This is secondary historiography, not a primary source. It is nevertheless directly relevant to the historical continuity question and should be represented as Grant's documented historical thesis.

## Empirical restraint

Medieval natural philosophy was not simply modern empirical science before modernity. Specialist work on medieval empiricism identifies meaningful experience-based epistemic constraints while also showing substantial differences from later experimental practice.

Aquinas, for example, recognizes limits on demonstration for phenomena not manifest to the senses and permits merely possible explanations where certainty is unavailable. This is methodologically relevant but should not be inflated into a claim that Aquinas practiced modern hypothesis testing.

## Revised continuity thesis

Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.

> Latin Christian intellectual culture did more than preserve isolated scientific facts. Medieval universities institutionalized sustained natural philosophy; Christian theologians developed accounts of an ordered creation with genuine secondary causes; and natural philosophers used causal reasoning to investigate nature, criticize inherited authorities, and consider alternative physical possibilities. These developments supplied significant intellectual and institutional antecedents to early modern natural philosophy, although the later experimental and mathematical sciences introduced important methodological transformations.

## Implication for CMD

The historical case no longer rests on a sequence of isolated Christian scientists.

A defensible continuity chain is emerging:

`Christian doctrine of created order and secondary causation -> medieval institutional natural philosophy -> early-modern theological/experimental natural philosophy -> shared Victorian scientific practice -> increasing naturalist institutional and interpretive dominance -> later formalization of methodological naturalism`

Each arrow denotes a research relationship, not an automatic claim of exclusive or monocausal descent.
