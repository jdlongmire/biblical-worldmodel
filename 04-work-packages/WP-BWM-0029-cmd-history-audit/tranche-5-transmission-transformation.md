# Tranche 5: Transmission and Transformation

Status: research synthesis, 2026-09-26

## Question

Can the medieval Christian-natural-philosophy antecedent be connected to early modern science by demonstrated institutional and intellectual transmission rather than by retrospective resemblance alone?

## Finding

Yes, with qualifications.

The transmission is strongest at the level of institutions, curricula, texts, questions, and categories. It is weaker if framed as a simple linear transmission of a single "Christian scientific method."

### 1. Greco-Islamic inheritance is indispensable

Edward Grant's historical synthesis explicitly describes modern science as cumulative across Greek, Islamic, and Latin civilizations. Latin Christendom received major scientific and philosophical resources through the twelfth- and thirteenth-century translation movement.

BWM should therefore reject any genealogy that treats medieval Latin science as creation ex nihilo. Christian intellectual culture received, translated, institutionalized, criticized, and developed inherited material.

### 2. Latin Christian institutionalization is also indispensable

By the thirteenth century, natural philosophy was embedded in European university Arts curricula. Aristotle's natural works became standard instructional material. Students proceeding to theology, medicine, law, and other learned vocations commonly passed through this philosophical curriculum.

This creates a concrete institutional transmission mechanism: natural-causal reasoning was reproduced across generations in durable Christian-European institutions rather than surviving only in isolated manuscripts or individuals.

### 3. Renaissance transformation retained continuity

Renaissance natural philosophy did not simply erase medieval university natural philosophy. Aristotelian natural philosophy remained central while new Platonic, atomist, Stoic, Neoplatonic, Hermetic, Copernican, mathematical, medical, and vernacular approaches expanded the field.

The Jesuit *Commentarii Conimbricenses*, beginning in the 1590s, is one clear example of late-sixteenth-century institutional continuity of Aristotelian natural philosophy inside a Christian educational system.

The transition to early modern science is therefore better represented as transformation, competition, recombination, and methodological expansion than as a clean rupture.

### 4. Mathematics and experiment are genuine transformations

Edward Grant emphasizes an important discontinuity: medieval natural philosophy and the exact mathematical sciences were substantially distinct disciplines. Their much deeper union in the seventeenth century was a major innovation.

Accordingly, medieval continuity should not be used to flatten the Scientific Revolution into "the Middle Ages continued." Early modern experimental practice, instrumentation, mathematical physics, print culture, geographic discovery, and competing philosophies materially transformed natural inquiry.

### 5. Biblical interpretation is a proposed causal contributor

Peter Harrison argues that changing approaches to biblical interpretation, especially Protestant literal/historical approaches, materially affected conceptions of nature and helped create conditions for early modern natural science.

This is significant secondary historiography because it proposes a mechanism by which specifically Christian hermeneutical developments affected natural inquiry, rather than merely observing that scientists were Christians.

It remains Harrison's historical thesis and should be compared with competing historiography before being made a canonical historical claim.

## Transmission model

The evidence supports the following qualified model:

`Greek natural philosophy + Islamic science/philosophy`
`                ↓ translation/reception`
`Latin Christian universities and theological assimilation`
`                ↓ curricular/institutional transmission`
`late-medieval and Renaissance natural philosophy`
`                ↓ transformation + rival traditions + print + mathematics`
`early-modern experimental/mathematical natural philosophy`
`                ↓ professional/institutional development`
`modern scientific disciplines`

Christian influence enters this chain in several distinguishable ways:

- institutional sponsorship and university structure;
- theological accommodation and authorization of natural causal inquiry;
- doctrines of creation, contingency, law/order, and secondary causation;
- scientific vocation and motivation in particular actors;
- biblical/hermeneutical changes proposed by Harrison and others;
- later confessional scientific networks and institutions.

## Disposition

### HIGH

There is real institutional and textual continuity between medieval university natural philosophy and Renaissance/early-modern natural philosophy.

### HIGH

Greco-Islamic inheritance is an indispensable antecedent and must be represented in any responsible BWM historical narrative.

### MEDIUM-HIGH

Latin Christian institutions did more than passively preserve inherited material: they institutionalized, taught, disputed, modified, and transmitted natural philosophy across generations.

### MEDIUM

Specific Christian theological commitments were among the causal contributors to the emergence of early modern science. This is supported by figure-level primary evidence and substantial specialist historiography, but the magnitude and mechanisms vary and remain historiographically contested.

### REJECT

A rupture model in which modern scientific reasoning appears only after natural philosophy escaped Christian intellectual culture.

### REJECT

An exclusivist model in which Christianity alone invented modern science without substantial Greek, Islamic, Renaissance, technological, mathematical, and other contributions.

## Revised CMD historical thesis

> Modern science arose through a long, transformed inheritance rather than from a single philosophical source. Greek and Islamic learning supplied indispensable intellectual resources. Latin Christian civilization translated, institutionalized, taught, contested, and developed natural philosophy within durable universities and theological frameworks. Early modern investigators then transformed that inheritance through new instruments, mathematical methods, experimental practices, competing philosophies, geographic discovery, print culture, and changing biblical and theological interpretations. Christian design commitments were therefore historically significant contributors within this development, while neither exhausting its causes nor reducing to the private identities of individual scientists.
