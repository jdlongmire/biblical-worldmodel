# Tranche 3: Continuity, Victorian Transition, and Naturalism

Status: research note, 2026-09-26

## Result

The continuity thesis is becoming more defensible, but it must be stated at the correct resolution.

The evidence supports a historical pattern in which theistic natural philosophy and later scientific naturalism shared substantial operational and methodological commitments. In nineteenth-century Britain, uniformity of natural law, hypothesis and theory, intellectual freedom, and the moral/scientific vocation were not uniquely naturalistic possessions. Theistic scientists used and justified these commitments within explicitly theological frameworks.

Specialist historiography further supports an institutional and interpretive transition in which scientific naturalists gained influence over scientific education and recast the history and foundations of science in increasingly naturalistic terms.

This supports a qualified version of the source paper's intuition while requiring revision of its rhetoric.

## Defensible formulation

> Christian theistic and design-oriented natural philosophy was not a peripheral exception to the development of modern science. It materially contributed to the motivations, expectations, practices, institutions, and intellectual frameworks within which modern natural science developed. During the nineteenth century, scientific naturalists inherited many operational practices and methodological values already shared with theistic scientists while increasingly relocating their justification within a naturalistic framework and gaining institutional influence over scientific education and professional identity.

This formulation is a historical synthesis supported at present primarily by specialist historiography plus the primary-source figure studies in earlier tranches.

## What should not be claimed yet

- that Christianity was the sole cause of modern science;
- that every important scientific method originated as a theological deduction;
- that all Victorian naturalists consciously appropriated methods from Christians;
- that "methodological naturalism" existed under that modern name throughout the nineteenth century;
- that the Victorian transition can be reduced to a single philosophical victory.

## Methodological naturalism terminology

The phrase **methodological naturalism** is substantially later than the Victorian transition. Ronald Numbers attributes its coinage to philosopher Paul de Vries in the 1980s, distinguishing disciplinary method from metaphysical naturalism.

Therefore BWM should distinguish:

1. naturalistic scientific practice or scientific naturalism in earlier periods;
2. historical exclusion or marginalization of divine agency from scientific explanation;
3. the later technical label **methodological naturalism**.

Using the modern label retroactively without qualification risks anachronism.

## Victorian transition

Matthew Stanley's specialist historical work is particularly important. His Maxwell/Huxley comparison argues that theistic and naturalistic scientists shared major methodological values and that uniformity of natural law was justified from both worldviews. Stanley further argues that naturalists' later dominance involved institutional strategies in scientific education and reinterpretation of disciplinary history, not demonstration that theistic science was operationally incapable of doing modern science.

This does not prove every element of CMD. It does materially undermine a simplistic history in which naturalistic methodology first supplied the operational practices that made modern science possible.

## Medieval continuity: status

The medieval-to-early-modern continuity claim remains open in this tranche. It requires direct work on representative scholastic treatments of secondary causation, contingency, natural regularity, and the autonomy of created causes.

The audit should prioritize Aquinas and selected late-medieval natural philosophers rather than rely on a generalized "medieval Christians invented secondary causes" claim.

## Provisional historical thesis

Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH for the nineteenth-century component; MEDIUM overall pending medieval continuity research.

> The rise of scientific naturalism should be modeled as a change in metaphysical interpretation, institutional authority, and explanatory admissibility occurring across a scientific culture whose operational practices were substantially shared with theistic practitioners, rather than as the replacement of a non-scientific Christian method by a uniquely naturalistic scientific method.
