# Abiogenesis Integrated Systems Compatibility Matrix — Initial Architecture

Status: referral artifact for WP-BWM-0027; methodological ownership remains WP-BWM-0029.

## Why this survives the harvest

Contemporary origin-of-life literature itself recognizes that life requires integration of replication, metabolism and compartmentalization, and that subsystem integration remains a major research problem. At the same time, experimental work has demonstrated common chemistry capable of yielding precursor families for nucleotides, amino acids and lipids.

Therefore the defensible BWM research question is neither "nothing has been accomplished" nor "abiogenesis is solved."

It is:

> Can experimentally demonstrated prebiotic subsystems be connected into a physically continuous, autonomous trajectory under mutually compatible environmental conditions?

## System-level gates

A proposed pathway must eventually close these interfaces:

1. precursor supply;
2. concentration;
3. selective synthesis;
4. polymerization;
5. persistence/degradation;
6. compartment formation;
7. membrane permeability and nutrient/waste transport;
8. usable energy coupling;
9. replication;
10. heredity;
11. translation/code or alternative route to encoded protein synthesis;
12. error tolerance;
13. open-ended Darwinian evolution.

## Literature-grounded corrections to intake source

Patel et al. (2015) experimentally produced precursor families for RNA, amino acids and lipids from a common cyanosulfidic chemistry. Therefore categorical claims that the required classes cannot arise through related prebiotic chemistry should be rejected.

However, Otto et al. (2020) describe functional integration of replication, metabolism and compartmentalization into an out-of-equilibrium system capable of open-ended Darwinian evolution as a major remaining challenge.

Ianeselli et al. (2023) further show that proposed prebiotic replication depends strongly on environmental parameters such as temperature, ions, pH, UV, CO2 and non-equilibrium geological settings. This makes boundary-condition compatibility a legitimate systems question.
