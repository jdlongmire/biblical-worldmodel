# James 1 Temptation and Sin-Boundary Pressure Test

Status: Research draft
Work package: WP-BWM-0028
Date: 2026-09-25
Primary text: James 1:12-18 (ESV)

## Purpose

Test the temptation -> consideration -> valuation -> assent -> willing -> enactment taxonomy against James 1:12-18, especially the distinction between God's relation to testing/temptation, creaturely desire, the conception of sin, and death.

## 1. Textual sequence

James presents several linked claims:

1. the person who remains steadfast under trial is blessed;
2. God is not temptable by evil;
3. God himself tempts no one with evil;
4. each person is tempted when lured and enticed by his own desire;
5. desire, after conceiving, gives birth to sin;
6. sin, when fully grown, brings forth death;
7. good giving comes from the Father;
8. God brings forth his people by the word of truth.

The passage therefore distinguishes God, trial/temptation, desire, sin, maturation, and death.

## 2. Peirasmos / peirazo discrimination

The James context requires a distinction between testing/trial and solicitation to evil.

A circumstance may function as a test of steadfastness without God being the morally evil tempter who solicits rebellion.

Therefore:

DivineOrdination(test circumstance) != DivineTemptationToEvil.

This directly constrains the divine-authorship model.

## 3. "Each person" and personal locus

James places the proximate moral locus of temptation in the person:

own desire -> lure/enticement.

This strongly supports personal attribution. The text does not locate the morally disordered desire in God.

For post-Fall persons, this integrates naturally with K_aff and K_noetic: inherited disorder conditions desire and valuation, while personally attributable response remains morally relevant.

For Adam, caution is required. James describes fallen hearers and cannot simply be projected backward as proof that Adam possessed preexisting disordered desire before the Fall.

## 4. Desire is not automatically identical to sin

James distinguishes desire from sin by using a generative sequence:

desire -> conception -> sin -> maturation -> death.

Therefore the mere existence of desire or attraction cannot simply be equated with completed sin.

This supports the taxonomy's discrimination between:

- perceived attractiveness / desire;
- culpable embrace or conception;
- sin;
- mature/enacted/persistent sin.

However, the metaphor should not be forced into a millisecond psychological chronology that James does not specify.

## 5. The conception boundary

The phrase "desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin" supplies the closest canonical analogue to the proposed T2 -> T3/T4 boundary.

The model should therefore refine its language:

T0 presentation
-> T1 temptation/consideration
-> T2 attraction/desire/valuation
-> T3 culpable conception/assent
-> T4 volitional rebellion
-> T5 enactment/persistence
-> mature sin/death.

The critical transition is not mere awareness of an attractive alternative. It is the point at which desire is culpably received, cultivated, assented to, or joined to the will such that rebellion is morally instantiated.

## 6. Christological control

Hebrews 4:15 remains necessary alongside James.

Christ is genuinely tempted yet without sin. Therefore:

Tempted(P) does not entail Sin(P).

James explains how temptation can progress to sin in fallen persons; Hebrews demonstrates that temptation need not do so.

Together they rule out:

temptation = sin.

## 7. Matthew 5 control

Matthew 5 prevents the opposite error.

Sin cannot be postponed until external bodily execution. Hatred/lustful intention can already instantiate culpable inward disorder.

Therefore:

ExternalAct(P) is not a necessary condition for Sin(P).

The boundary must lie within morally attributable assent/intention/willing, even though outward action may embody and intensify the sin.

## 8. Genesis 3 application

Genesis 3:6 narrates a progression involving perception and valuation: the woman sees the tree in relation to food, visual desirability, and wisdom, then takes and eats.

The narrative supports distinguishing perceived goods from enacted disobedience.

The text does not provide enough psychological granularity to identify the exact instant of inward culpable assent before taking/eating.

Therefore WP-BWM-0028 should not claim that Genesis explicitly locates sin at T3. Rather:

- Genesis supplies the historical sequence of temptation, valuation, and action;
- James supplies a desire/conception/sin progression;
- Matthew 5 establishes inward culpability before external action;
- Hebrews 4 establishes temptation without sin.

The taxonomy is a canonical synthesis across these texts.

## 9. Adam and self-interest

For Adam, the relevant created goods can remain genuine goods:

- self-preservation;
- wisdom;
- fulfillment;
- created enjoyment;
- relational goods;
- rational deliberation.

The moral disorder occurs when perceived self-good is treated autonomously over God's word and loving communion.

Thus:

natural self-love != sin.
ordinary self-interest != sin.
consideration of perceived self-good != sin.
autonomous self-interest governing against God -> disordered assent/willing -> sin.

## 10. Divine-authorship consequence

James explicitly blocks a formulation in which God is the evil tempter.

The model therefore distinguishes:

Father decrees/ordains the history
!=
Father solicits Adam to evil
!=
Father possesses Adam's disordered intention.

The Father's sovereign ordination can include a test/temptation circumstance while the morally evil solicitation and culpable assent are not attributed to God.

For Genesis 3, the serpent is the creaturely tempter in the narrative. Adam and Eve remain the human agents who evaluate and act.

## 11. Refined boundary proposition

The earlier proposition should be sharpened to:

> Temptation is the presentation or apprehension of a possible disobedient good and may include experienced attraction without itself constituting sin. Sin begins when the moral agent culpably conceives, embraces, or assents to the disordered good such that the agent's own will prefers that good contrary to God's rightful ordering, whether or not outward enactment has yet occurred.

This keeps "dwelling" only where dwelling means culpable cultivation/assent. Mere involuntary recurrence of a tempting thought is not thereby classified as sin.

## 12. Important pastoral/anthropological guardrail

The term "dwelling" can become imprecise if it makes duration the criterion.

Duration is not the essential distinction.

A fleeting but fully willed malicious intention may be sinful. A persistent intrusive temptation resisted by the agent may remain temptation rather than culpable assent.

Therefore the governing category is **voluntary moral assent**, not elapsed mental duration.

This distinction should govern later treatment of noetic effects, intrusive cognition, temptation, and culpability.

## 13. Revised taxonomy

| Stage | Description | Status |
|---|---|---|
| T0 | Alternative presented | Not sin |
| T1 | Alternative apprehended / temptation experienced | Not sin |
| T2 | Attraction, desire, or perceived good recognized | Not necessarily sin |
| T3 | Desire culpably conceived/cultivated; disordered good embraced | Sin boundary |
| T4 | Will prefers autonomous self-good over God's order/communion | Sin |
| T5 | External enactment | Sin embodied |
| T6 | Persistence, rationalization, hardening | Sin reinforced/matured |

## 14. Result

The canonical synthesis supports the category discrimination the model requires:

**temptation is not sin; inward culpable assent can be sin before external action.**

James strengthens the first-sin account by providing a desire -> conception -> sin progression while explicitly denying that God tempts anyone with evil.

The principal refinement is that "dwelling" should not be defined temporally. The morally relevant boundary is culpable assent, cultivation, embrace, or willing of the disordered good.

## 15. Disposition

- Temptation = sin: **REJECTED**.
- External action required before sin exists: **REJECTED**.
- Mere attraction/desire always equals sin: **REJECTED as too coarse**.
- Culpable inward assent/willing as sin boundary: **SUPPORTED as canonical synthesis**.
- God as evil tempter: **REJECTED**.
- Exact psychological instant of Adam's first inward sin: **NOT SPECIFIED by Genesis; model-level synthesis remains appropriate**.
