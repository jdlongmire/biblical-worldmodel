# Divine Authorship of Sin Pressure Test

Status: Research draft
Work package: WP-BWM-0028
Date: 2026-09-25

## Question

If the Father comprehensively decrees and ordains the whole moral history of creation, including the certainty of the Fall and subsequent sinful acts, does it follow that God is the author or morally culpable agent of sin?

This pressure test may not weaken comprehensive decree, deny genuine creaturely agency, equate inherited noetic corruption with personal guilt, or invoke "mystery" to conceal a contradiction.

## 1. Terms that must remain distinct

### Decree

The Father's free, comprehensive, certain, and unfailing determination of the history and end that shall obtain.

### Ordination

The Father's ordering of the means, events, boundaries, relations, and consequences through which the decreed history obtains.

### Sustenance

God's continuing preservation of created persons and their powers of action.

### Creaturely agency

The genuine exercise of derivative personal willing, intention, deliberation, and action by an image-bearing creature.

### Intention

The end, meaning, or good toward which an agent personally directs an act.

### Sinful moral predicate

The property by which a creaturely intention or act is rebellion, unbelief, disordered love, lawlessness, or violation of rightful communion.

### Culpability

Personal moral liability attaching to the agent whose culpable willing and act instantiate the sinful moral predicate.

None of these terms may be substituted for another without argument.

## 2. Strong form of the objection

The objection should be stated at full strength:

1. The Father decrees and ordains every event in the actual moral history.
2. Adam's Fall and every later sin are events in that history.
3. Therefore the Father decrees and ordains their certain occurrence.
4. Without God's decree, creation, sustenance, and providence, the creature and act would not occur.
5. Therefore God appears to be the ultimate sufficient explanation for the sinful event.
6. If ultimate sufficient explanation entails moral authorship, God appears to be morally responsible for sin.
7. If God instead lacks control over the sinful event, comprehensive sovereignty appears false.

The model must reject the inference at step 6 without retreating from steps 1-5.

## 3. Canonical control: Genesis 50:20

Joseph's statement assigns two morally different intentions to the same providential history:

- the brothers mean evil;
- God means the event for good.

The text therefore blocks the inference:

same event -> same intention -> same moral predicate.

God's providential intention is not a morally neutral absence of intention. It is positively good.

This gives a canonical dual-intention rule:

> One historical event can be comprehended within divine good intention while simultaneously being enacted through creaturely evil intention.

## 4. Canonical control: Acts 2:23

The crucifixion is simultaneously:

- according to God's definite plan and foreknowledge; and
- performed through culpable human agents.

The text does not solve every metaphysical question, but it requires any adequate model to preserve both predicates.

A model that saves creaturely culpability by reducing the divine plan to observation fails the text.

A model that saves divine sovereignty by making the human agents morally fictitious also fails the text.

## 5. Canonical control: Acts 4:27-28

Herod, Pontius Pilate, Gentiles, and Israel are named as actual agents. Their actions occur within what God's hand and plan predestined to take place.

The event most central to redemption therefore supplies the strongest canonical case of:

comprehensive divine ordination + genuine creaturely agency + creaturely culpability + divine righteous purpose.

The synthesis is not an ad hoc device invented only for Adam.

## 6. Logical structure

Let event e include creaturely act a by P.

The model affirms:

D(e)
O(e)
Sustain(G,P)
AgentOf(P,a)
Intends(P,a,evil)
Intends(G,e,good)

It denies:

Intends(P,a,evil) -> Intends(G,e,evil)

and denies:

O(e) -> AgentOf(G,a-as-creaturely-sin)

The divine and creaturely predicates operate at distinct personal and intentional levels while concerning one actual history.

## 7. Why ordination is not moral authorship

Moral authorship in the culpable sense would require the sinful moral predicate to belong to God: disordered love, rebellion against rightful authority, falsehood, malice, or another defect.

But the Father has no higher rightful authority against whom he rebels, no disordered self-love, no false conception of the good, and no defect in intention.

The Father's intention in ordaining a history containing creaturely sin is therefore not identical to the creature's intention in committing the sin.

This does not mean God's relationship to the event is merely passive. The model affirms active decree and ordination. The moral distinction rests in personal agency, intention, and moral predicate, not in converting decree into permission-only language.

## 8. Why creaturely agency is not independence

A possible escape would make creaturely willing metaphysically independent of God. WP-BWM-0028 rejects that move.

P is derivative.
P's powers are created.
P is sustained by God.
P's historical circumstances fall within providence.
P's act occurs within the decreed history.

Yet derivative does not mean fictitious.

The act may genuinely be P's act even though P is neither self-existent nor outside providence.

The relevant ownership claim is:

> Dependence of the creature upon God does not entail identity of the creature's personal willing with God's willing.

Otherwise Creator-creature distinction collapses.

## 9. The sinful intention locus

The phrase "the moral agent supplies the sinful intention" must be interpreted carefully.

It does not mean the creature creates ex nihilo an autonomous causal contribution outside providence.

It means:

- the willing is genuinely the creature's willing;
- the intention is genuinely the creature's intention;
- the disordered orientation of that intention is predicated of the creature;
- the creature acts voluntarily according to that disordered orientation;
- culpability therefore truthfully attaches to the creature.

A safer canonical formulation is:

> The sinful moral quality of the creaturely act belongs to the creature's own disordered intention and willing; the Father's intention in ordaining the history is holy, righteous, and good.

## 10. Noetic effects and culpability

The post-Fall analysis must preserve:

K_noetic(P) != Q(P)

Inherited noetic disorder conditions P's knowing, interpreting, and valuing. It does not itself make P personally culpable for Adam's intention.

For the ordinary accountable fallen person:

inherited noetic/affective disorder
-> encounter with apprehensible truth/good
-> personally attributable suppression or disordered willing
-> actual rebellion
-> individual culpability.

Thus God is not made the culpable agent by being sovereign over the existence of K, and the creature is not made innocent merely because K is inherited.

The morally relevant question remains whose culpable willing instantiates the rebellion.

## 11. Does decretive certainty destroy responsibility?

The objection may be restated:

If □D(P does a), P cannot do otherwise; therefore P is not responsible.

This assumes the Principle of Alternative Possibilities.

WP-BWM-0028 does not grant that principle as constitutive of responsibility.

The relevant condition is whether a proceeds from P's own willing, intention, loves, and deliberation rather than being an externally compelled movement contrary to P's will.

Divine freedom supplies a control: God's inability to sin does not make God unfree.

Glorified agency supplies a proposed creaturely control: perfected persons remain genuine agents without apostasy.

Therefore inability to actualize a contrary sinful alternative does not by itself establish absence of agency.

## 12. Does God "cause" sin?

The word "cause" is too underspecified to carry the argument.

At least these questions must be separated:

1. Is God the ontological cause of the creature's existence and powers? Yes.
2. Does God sustain the creature during the act? Yes.
3. Does the event fall within decree and ordination? Yes.
4. Does God ordain that the event certainly occurs? Yes, under the model.
5. Is the creature a genuine secondary/derivative agent? Yes.
6. Is the creature's sinful intention genuinely the creature's? Yes.
7. Does God possess the sinful intention or moral defect? No.
8. Is God therefore the culpable sinner? No, unless one can establish that causal ultimacy alone transfers the creature's moral predicate to God.

The objection therefore requires an additional moral-transfer principle. It cannot obtain the conclusion merely from the word "cause."

## 13. Moral-transfer principle pressure test

Candidate principle:

> If X is the ultimate sufficient explanation for the occurrence of Y's voluntary sinful act, then the sinful moral predicate of Y's act transfers to X.

This principle is not self-evident.

It would need to show why:

- personal distinction is irrelevant;
- intention is irrelevant;
- derivative agency is not genuine;
- moral predicates transfer upward through causal dependence;
- the canonical dual-intention cases do not block the transfer.

Until defended, the principle begs the central question.

WP-BWM-0028 therefore rejects automatic moral transfer while leaving open deeper work on the exact metaphysics of divine and creaturely causation.

## 14. Hard cases

### Adam

Adam is created good, is a genuine agent, and his Fall is decretively certain but not logically necessary. His sinful intention is his own. The model still owes a fuller account of how the first disordered intention arises in a good creature without making God the sinful intentional agent.

This pressure point is now narrowed by `temptation-first-sin-boundary.md`: Adam requires no preexisting sinful substance or inherited noetic corruption. The morally relevant transition is from temptation/consideration of an alternative perceived good to culpable disordered assent and willing, where autonomous self-interest is elevated over God's word and loving communion. The deeper metaphysics of how a good mutable creature reaches that assent within a decretively certain history remains open.

### Crucifixion

The crucifixion demonstrates the strongest positive case for ordained sinful action serving a righteous divine purpose without the human actors becoming innocent.

### Post-Fall sin

Inherited noetic and affective corruption makes the dispositional structure different from Adam's pre-Fall case. The corruption conditions the fallen person's willing, but personal culpability attaches to personally attributable rebellion.

### Election and non-election

If the Father effectually rescues some guilty persons and does not effectually rescue all, the moral question becomes whether mercy is owed. The model says it is not. This claim must remain connected to the justice of individualized judgment rather than used as a slogan.

## 15. Current result

The proposition:

"God decrees and ordains a sinful event"

does **not logically entail**:

"God is the sinful moral agent of that event."

The entailment fails unless an additional premise transfers moral predicates across distinct agents merely because one agent is the ultimate sovereign ground of the history.

Canonical dual-intention texts provide positive warrant for distinguishing divine purpose from creaturely sinful intention.

Therefore the divine-authorship objection does not currently establish a contradiction in WP-BWM-0028.

However, two problems remain open:

1. the metaphysics of the first sinful intention in Adam; and
2. the exact relation between divine ordination, creaturely secondary causation, and alternative possibilities.

## 16. Disposition

**Divine-authorship contradiction: NOT ESTABLISHED.**

**Compatibility of comprehensive decree with genuine creaturely culpability: STRONGLY SUPPORTED canonically, with metaphysical details still requiring work.**

**God as culpable moral author of sin: REJECTED within the model because sinful intention and moral defect are not predicated of God.**

**First-sin origination problem: OPEN.**

This is not a claim that every philosophical problem of providence has been solved. It is the narrower finding that comprehensive decree plus creaturely sin does not, without additional premises, entail divine culpability.
