## Abstract

A longstanding objection to reading the creation days of Genesis 1 as ordinary days holds that the events of Genesis 2:7–25, and especially Adam's naming of the animals, cannot fit within the sixth day. This paper tests that objection by constructing an explicit, text-constrained chronological model. It argues that the *toledot* structure of Genesis 2:4 presents chapter 2 as an elaboration of the sixth day; that the pluperfect sense of Genesis 2:19 is grammatically defensible and independently supported by the day order of Genesis 1; that Genesis 2:20 restricts the named animals to livestock, birds, and beasts of the field; and that published creationist estimates of created kinds place the relevant set at roughly 500 to 1,000. A procession of approximately five hours then allows eighteen to thirty-six seconds per kind, without appeal to superhuman capacities, and leaves ample time for the remaining events of the day. The principal objections, drawn chiefly from old-earth creationist literature, are addressed in turn. The paper concludes that the objection from impossibility fails, while the substantive dispute concerns the depth of deliberation the naming required, a question the text does not settle.

**Keywords:** Genesis 2; creation days; naming of the animals; baraminology; created kinds; *wayyiqtol*; young-earth creationism

## 1. Introduction

Genesis 1 places the creation of man and woman on the sixth day. Genesis 2 describes the formation of the man, his placement in Eden, the naming of the animals, and the building of the woman. Critics commonly treat the two chapters as independent and conflicting accounts. Among interpreters who read them as complementary, a further dispute arises: whether the events of Genesis 2 can occur within a single ordinary day.

Old-earth creationists have pressed this question as the "argument from the sixth day." Campbell (2014) states it plainly: too many events occur on the sixth day to be compressed into twenty-four hours. He cites Archer, Collins, Schaeffer, Geisler, and Ross among those persuaded by the argument, and notes that Bavinck, while conceding the events could have occurred within a few hours, judged that unlikely (Campbell 2014, citing Bavinck 2004, p. 500).

Young-earth responses have typically offered brief arithmetic. Sarfati, for example, proposes that even 2,500 kinds at five seconds each could be named in well under four hours (Sarfati 2011, pp. 89–90, as cited in Campbell 2014). This paper develops that line of reply into a fuller model. It proceeds in five steps: the literary relationship between the chapters, the grammar of Genesis 2:19, the scope of the animals named in 2:20, the number of created kinds that scope implies, and the resulting timeline. Objections are then addressed in sequence.

The model does not claim to reconstruct what happened. Its purpose is narrower: to determine whether a coherent sequence exists, constrained by the text and by current creationist taxonomy, in which all of Genesis 2:7–25 occurs within the sixth day. If one does, the objection from impossibility fails, and the remaining disagreement can be located precisely.

## 2. The Literary Relationship of Genesis 1 and 2

Genesis 2:4 opens with the first *toledot* formula of the book: "These are the generations of the heavens and the earth when they were created." The formula recurs as a structural marker throughout Genesis (5:1; 6:9; 10:1) and characteristically introduces what proceeds from the preceding section. Genesis 2:4–25 therefore reads most naturally as an elaboration developing from the cosmic account, with its focus narrowed to man, the garden, and the covenant relationship signalled by the shift from *Elohim* to *YHWH Elohim*.

Collins (1995, pp. 138–139) argues that Genesis 2:4 is itself an elaborate chiasm binding the two accounts together. The phrase "the heavens and the earth" and the verb *bara'* point back to Genesis 1, while the divine name *YHWH Elohim* anticipates chapters 2–3. He describes the structure as evidence of art rather than coincidence, and concludes that the traditional reading of 2:4–25 as an elaboration of the sixth day is how an audience would cooperate with the author's intention (Collins 1995, p. 139).

The New Testament reads the chapters the same way. In Matthew 19:4–5, Jesus joins Genesis 1:27 ("made them male and female") and Genesis 2:24 ("the two shall become one flesh") into a single argument. Whatever one concludes about sources, the canonical text presents the chapters as complementary. Old-earth interpreters share this premise: Campbell (2014) likewise treats Genesis 2:5–25 as a detailed recap of the sixth day from man's point of view. The dispute therefore concerns duration, and the harmonisation itself is common ground.

## 3. The Grammar of Genesis 2:19

### 3.1 The pluperfect reading

Read as simple narrative sequence, Genesis 2:19 appears to place the formation of the animals after the man, contradicting Genesis 1. The ESV renders the verb as a pluperfect: "Now out of the ground the LORD God *had formed* every beast of the field and every bird of the heavens."

The verb is a *wayyiqtol*, the form ordinarily used for sequential narrative. Collins (1995) examines whether the *wayyiqtol* can, without a preceding perfect, denote an event prior to the previous verb. Surveying the classical treatments, he argues that it can under identifiable discourse conditions, with Genesis 2:19 among the relevant cases. Waltke and O'Connor's syntax likewise allows the form to carry a pluperfect sense (Waltke and O'Connor 1990, pp. 552–553, as cited in Kruger 2023). Among commentators, Leupold judged that it would not be wrong to translate *yatsar* here as a pluperfect (Leupold 1942, p. 130, as cited in Lyons 2002), and Hamilton acknowledged that "had formed" is a possible translation, though he himself took 2:19 to describe a special group of animals created for the naming (Hamilton 1990, p. 176, as cited in Lyons 2002).

### 3.2 Divergence among interpreters

The pluperfect reading is contested. Collins (1995, p. 138) records Buth's position that the pluperfect rendering of Genesis 2 is harmonistic exegesis, and answers that the database from which Buth inferred his syntactic rules was too small. Cassuto rejected the pluperfect and harmonised the verse differently: of the species already created, God "now formed particular specimens for the purpose of presenting them all before man in the midst of the Garden" (Cassuto 1961, p. 129, as cited in Lyons 2002; see also Mathews 1996, p. 215 n., as cited in Peachey 2021).

For the present model the conclusion is modest. The pluperfect is grammatically defensible and supported by credentialed Hebraists, though not uncontested. The chronology does not depend on it alone, since the alternatives offered by Cassuto and Hamilton also place the event within the sixth day.

## 4. The Manner of Presentation

It has occasionally been suggested that God "brought" the animals to Adam in a mental or visionary presentation, which would remove any logistical difficulty. The text weighs against this reading.

The verb in 2:19 is the hiphil of *bo'*, "caused to come." The same form appears in 2:22, where God builds the woman and "brought her to the man." The second bringing is unambiguously physical, and the narrator uses the identical verb within the same scene. Hebrew possesses idioms for bringing something to mind, such as a thing "coming up on the heart" (Jeremiah 3:16; Isaiah 65:17), and none is used here. The concreteness of the surrounding narrative (dust, breath, sleep, rib, flesh) reinforces a bodily encounter.

A mental presentation would also relieve little of the time pressure, since observing and naming consume time whether the creatures stand in the garden or appear in the mind. The model therefore assumes a physical presentation. Campbell (2014) proposes an alternative physical mode in which God led Adam through the animals' habitats. The text's language of bringing the animals *to* the man, and its parallel with the woman being brought *to* the man, favours a procession, though nothing in the model's arithmetic depends on the choice.

## 5. The Scope of Genesis 2:20

Genesis 1:24–25 lists the land animals of the sixth day in three categories: livestock (*behemah*), creeping things (*remes*), and beasts of the earth. Genesis 2:20 lists the animals Adam named: "all livestock and... the birds of the heavens and... every beast of the field." Creeping things are absent, and birds, created on the fifth day, are included. Sarfati makes the same observation, adding that "beasts of the field" is plausibly a subset of the "beasts of the earth" of 1:24 (Sarfati 2011, pp. 89–90, as cited in Campbell 2014).

Three consequences follow. First, sea creatures fall outside the naming entirely. Second, creeping things are plausibly excluded. Leviticus 11:29–30 places the lizard types among "the swarming things that swarm on the ground," which supports treating small reptiles and similar ground creatures as outside the named categories. The boundaries of these Hebrew terms are not sharply defined, so this exclusion is probable rather than certain. Third, because birds were made on the fifth day and land animals earlier on the sixth, every creature in the procession already existed before Adam was formed. The day order thus independently supports the pluperfect sense of 2:19.

Lightner et al. (2011) note that "after its kind" and "according to its kind" translate a single Hebrew phrase appearing in Genesis 1, 6, and 7, and argue that the text supports treating each kind as a reproductive unit. Arment (2022) situates the question within ethnobiological classification, observing that Leviticus 11 itself constitutes a classification scheme and that creation researchers have given folk taxonomy relatively little attention. This matters for Genesis 2:20, whose categories are functional and observational and should not be forced into modern taxonomic ranks. The Ark Kinds programme itself employs the cognitum, a group of organisms naturally grouped together through human perception (Sanders and Wise 2003; Lightner et al. 2011). Naming by an observer presented with living creatures is, in effect, the recognition of cognita, which suggests that Adam's names operated at a perceptual level of classification accessible without prolonged study.

## 6. Quantifying the Kinds

### 6.1 The Ark Kinds research programme

Beginning in 2011, *Answers Research Journal* published a series of papers estimating the number of land-dwelling, air-breathing kinds preserved on the Ark. The programme gives primary weight to hybridisation data, supplemented by the cognitum and statistical baraminology (Lightner et al. 2011). The broader methodological framework for identifying created kinds is set out in Wood and Murray (2003). Arment (2020) summarises the programme's results as follows:

| Group | Estimate | Primary source |
|---|---|---|
| Birds (extant) | 196 kinds | Lightner 2013 |
| Mammals (extant and fossil) | up to 300 kinds | Lightner 2012 |
| Reptiles (extant) | 101 kinds | Hennigan 2014a, 2014b, 2015 |
| Amphibians (extant and fossil) | 248 kinds | Hennigan 2013a, 2013b; Ross 2014a |

Ark Encounter (n.d.) reports a combined total of 1,398 living and extinct kinds of land animals and flying creatures, and Ham (2022) notes the true figure could be closer to a thousand. Beech and Ross (2011) offer an independent re-estimation.

### 6.2 Adjusting the count to Genesis 2:20

The Ark total is an upper bound for the naming, since it includes the creeping things of Genesis 7:14. Removing amphibians (248) and the lepidosaurs (lizards, snakes, tuataras, and amphisbaenians, which comprise most of the 101 extant reptile kinds) reduces the total substantially. The remaining named groups are mammals, birds, and the larger reptiles, including extinct forms.

Two further considerations apply. The bird estimate covers extant kinds only, so fossil bird kinds would add to the count. And Arment's (2022) distinction between created kinds and Ark kinds suggests that kinds may have diversified between creation and the Flood. On the sixth day no diversification had yet occurred, which implies the created set was, if anything, smaller.

A reasonable range for the named kinds is therefore approximately 500 to 1,000. No published study scopes a count to Genesis 2:20 specifically, and this range should be treated as a working estimate. It is adequate for testing feasibility, since the conclusion holds even at the full Ark figure (§8.2).

## 7. Dinosaurs and the Boundary of Kinds

### 7.1 The model's position

The present model groups crocodilians and non-avian dinosaurs as a single created kind, distinct from a lizard kind, and treats birds as separate. The separation of birds rests first on the text: birds belong to the fifth day, land animals to the sixth. The grouping of dinosaurs with crocodilians rests on shared design features, including socketed teeth and skull architecture, which place both on the archosaur side of the reptiles and apart from lizards. Hennigan (2014a) records the conventional subdivision of the reptiles into Archosauromorpha (crocodiles, dinosaurs, birds, and possibly turtles) and Lepidosauromorpha (tuataras, lizards, amphisbaenians, and snakes).

Similarity of design does not by itself establish common descent. A common-design interpretation reads the same morphological data as reflecting a shared plan. Whether the data are read by descent or by design, however, the similarity pattern places dinosaurs nearer crocodilians than lizards, and the model follows that pattern.

### 7.2 Divergence from published baraminology

The single dinosaur-crocodilian kind departs significantly from the creationist literature. Ross (2014b), in the first statistical baraminological study to define a dinosaur holobaramin, concluded that Dinosauria, Saurischia, and Theropoda are each polybaraminic, meaning each contains more than one created kind. Doran et al. (2018), applying baraminic distance correlation, multidimensional scaling, and principal component analysis to eighteen character matrices, estimated 27 potential holobaramins among the dinosaur groups examined.

Two considerations bear on this divergence. First, statistical baraminology is itself debated among creationist researchers, and *Answers Research Journal* has published both critiques of baraminic distance correlation and defences of it as a useful heuristic. Second, the model's grouping draws partly on the breadth of the biblical categories, which are observational and need not map onto family-level taxa. A defender of the single-kind grouping must nonetheless argue that one created kind carried sufficient designed variability to encompass forms as disparate as sauropods and modern crocodiles. That argument lies beyond the scope of this paper.

For the timeline, the difference is marginal. Whether dinosaurs constitute one kind or twenty-seven, the effect on a total of 500 to 1,000 is small.

## 8. The Timing Model

### 8.1 Assumptions

The model rests on three stated parameters: a daylight period of approximately twelve hours, which the text does not specify; a procession lasting approximately five hours; and a named set of 500 to 1,000 kinds.

The five-hour duration is a chosen parameter. The model demonstrates that the events *can* fit within the day under reasonable assumptions; it does not demonstrate that the naming took five hours. The duration was selected to allow deliberate observation rather than rapid labelling, and the sensitivity analysis below shows that the conclusion does not depend on it.
