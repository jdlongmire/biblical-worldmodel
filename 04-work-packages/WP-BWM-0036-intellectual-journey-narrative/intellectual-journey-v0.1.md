# From Inherited Confidence to a Biblical World Model
## Biographical narrative, v0.1

I did not begin this journey trying to construct a new creation model. I began with a Bible I believed was true.

My upbringing was very much fundamentalist. I do not say that as a criticism. It gave me a durable confidence that Scripture speaks truthfully about the world God made and the history He governs. Genesis was not merely devotional literature or theological symbolism. God created. Adam and Eve were real. The Fall was real. The Flood was real history. The creation account described divine action in the real world.

Young creation was therefore the basic framework I inherited.

What that formation did not give me, at least in the form I received it, was a mature method for interrogating sophisticated challenges to those conclusions. I knew what I believed. I was less prepared to ask what assumptions governed the competing account, how its models were constructed, or where observation ended and historical reconstruction began.

My professional life was teaching me those questions from another direction.

I came up through information technology from the ground level, beginning as a network technician and maturing through architecture into enterprise architecture and systems engineering. Exposure to defense-system modeling and simulation added another layer. In those disciplines, abstractions eventually have to survive contact with systems.

A model is not the system.

Models have boundaries. They have assumptions. They have inputs, initial states, interfaces, constraints, abstractions, and permitted behaviors. A simulation can execute perfectly and still misrepresent the system because the error lies in the model rather than the computation. Verification asks whether we built the model correctly. Validation asks whether we built the right model for the real thing we are trying to understand.

Those habits of thought did not immediately become an origins methodology. They accumulated.

A major theological milestone came when I encountered the PCA's formal treatment of the creation question. Here was my own conservative Reformed tradition allowing serious interpretive space around the creation days while retaining a high doctrine of Scripture and the historical substance of Genesis. That mattered. It showed me that reconsidering my inherited interpretation did not itself require abandoning biblical authority.

So I began asking harder questions.

I engaged old-earth interpretations. I took Ancient Near Eastern frameworks seriously. I tried to understand the literary arguments surrounding Genesis rather than dismissing them. I took conventional cosmology, geology, radiometric dating, and the broader deep-time reconstruction seriously.

For a time, my project became one of harmonization.

Could I retain Scripture as authoritative while understanding Genesis in a way compatible with an old cosmos and old earth? Could the days be understood differently? Could literary structure or ANE context carry more interpretive weight than I had previously allowed? Could the scientific reconstruction of natural history and a faithful Christian systematic coexist?

I came to think they could.

Then something began to trouble me, and the problem first became visible in my own systematic theology.

No single accommodation seemed decisive. Many were individually plausible. Yet their cumulative direction began to show a pattern. When the conventional reconstruction of natural history and my reading of Scripture appeared to conflict, the pressure seemed consistently to run in one direction.

Scripture moved.

The external reconstruction rarely did.

That forced a question I had not originally set out to ask: what was actually regulating my interpretation?

I eventually coined a term for what I saw happening in myself: **presuppositional capitulation**.

By that I mean the progressive, often individually plausible, accommodation of one's systematic commitments to the governing assumptions of another worldview until those assumptions acquire functional authority over the original system.

The phrase is intentionally stronger than influence. I was trying to describe an erosion that can occur while someone continues sincerely to affirm biblical authority. One can affirm inspiration, inerrancy, creation, miracles, the Resurrection, and divine sovereignty while quietly allowing another framework to establish the rules for what Scripture may plausibly mean.

That does not make every old-earth Christian guilty of presuppositional capitulation. The term arose as a diagnosis of my own reasoning. An old-earth interpretation could be argued exegetically. The question that began pressing on me concerned the direction of authority.

When Scripture and an external historical reconstruction appear to conflict, which framework is permitted to challenge the assumptions of the other?

That question changed the journey.

I increasingly found the creation debate presented through two recognizable camps. Ken Ham represented a familiar young-earth package: ordinary creation days, recent creation, global Flood, and resistance to deep-time reconstruction. Hugh Ross represented a serious old-earth alternative: an ancient cosmos and earth combined with special creation and a strong claim to biblical fidelity.

I could see things I wanted to retain from both conversations. I shared major biblical conclusions associated with young creation, yet I did not want to inherit every scientific auxiliary or apologetic argument commonly attached to YEC. I wanted serious engagement with empirical evidence, yet I was no longer prepared to grant the conventional deep-time reconstruction the status of neutral observation.

I began asking myself a simple question:

**Is there a third way outside the Ken Ham and Hugh Ross camps?**

That question turned out to be more radical than I initially realized.

I was not really looking for a midpoint.

I was beginning to question the axis.

Could I take biblical history with full material seriousness and empirical evidence with full intellectual seriousness? Could I distinguish what we observe from the historical model through which we reconstruct an unobserved past? Could I expose initialization conditions and boundary assumptions instead of burying them? Could supernatural divine action remain an admissible historical cause where Scripture independently warrants that God acted? Could I do all of that without turning “God did it” into an escape from explanatory accountability?

My systems background now became directly relevant.

What is the observed state? What is inferred from it? What model connects them? What initial conditions have been specified? Which variables are measured and which are derived? Which causal mechanisms are admitted? Which have been excluded before the model begins? How sensitive is the conclusion to those choices? Are multiple apparent lines of confirmation genuinely independent, or do they inherit common assumptions? And are we confusing the output of the model with the history of the system itself?

Creation, considered seriously as creation, is also an initialization problem.

The laws governing an operating system do not necessarily specify how that system was initialized. The running state of a network does not contain its deployment history. The behavior of software after startup does not tell us how its initial data was populated. A simulation beginning from a specified state does not entail that the simulated system arrived at that state through the dynamics subsequently modeled.

That insight became one of the roots of what I later called **Designed Functional Maturity**.

If God created functioning systems, then their initialized states matter to historical reconstruction. Extrapolating ordinary post-initialization processes backward through the initialization boundary may produce a coherent retrodictive history without establishing that the retrodicted history actually occurred.

That insight creates a danger of its own. Initialization can become an all-purpose answer to inconvenient evidence. If so, it ceases to explain anything.

My response has been to make supernaturalist models carry explanatory burdens too. Ordinary post-creation processes must be investigated. Historically warranted catastrophes must be investigated where they possess relevant causal capacity. Creation-process events must be considered. Initialization belongs within an ordered explanatory structure rather than serving as a universal escape clause.

This developed into what I now call **regulative supernaturalism**:

> **If Scripture warrants supernatural divine action in the history of creation, explanations of the resulting world must permit that action to function as a genuine historical cause.**

The word *regulative* matters. Supernaturalism establishes the admissible causal space under revelation. It does not tell us in advance which explanation wins.

Another stream was developing alongside this one: my iterative exposure to intellectual exemplars.

Christ the Logos remained the governing center. The apostolic witness supplied the authoritative Scriptural revelation by which everything else had to be tested. But I increasingly encountered thinkers who gave me tools for questions I was already learning to ask.

Cornelius Van Til sharpened my recognition that no one reasons from neutral ground and helped me examine the presuppositions beneath methodological naturalism itself.

Aristotle reinforced categorical precision, causality, teleology, and the discipline of defining what kind of thing we are actually discussing before arguing about it.

Socrates reinforced the habit of asking the next question, exposing assumptions, and testing whether a conclusion actually follows.

Thomas Reid helped me retain confidence that human beings genuinely know an external world without requiring an impossible foundation of indubitable propositions.

Alvin Plantinga clarified warrant and helped break the assumption that Christian belief must first receive rational permission from an external naturalistic tribunal.

Thomas Aquinas, approached critically from within my Reformed commitments, demonstrated the intellectual seriousness of disciplined reasoning about metaphysics, causality, analogy, natural theology, and revelation.

Kurt Gödel reinforced the limits of formal systems and the danger of confusing rigorous representation with exhaustive reality.

Imre Lakatos gave me a way to think about research programmes rather than isolated arguments: hard cores, protective belts, heuristics, progressive development, degeneration, and the legitimate role of auxiliary hypotheses without allowing them to become an unfalsifiable shield.

Thomas Bayes supplied a disciplined language for comparing models under uncertainty. The important question was no longer how much evidence I could accumulate for my preferred view, but whether a particular observation was actually more expected under one model than another.

I did not adopt these thinkers as a composite magisterium. Their contributions entered iteratively. I tested them, retained what proved useful, qualified what required qualification, and rejected what conflicted with the governing authority of Scripture.

“Test everything; hold fast what is good” became more than a useful verse for intellectual inquiry. It described the method.

In retrospect, several streams had converged.

My fundamentalist upbringing gave me confidence in Scripture.

The PCA milestone gave me permission to question an inherited interpretation without assuming that the questioning itself was unbelief.

Old-earth and ANE engagement forced me to test the inherited model seriously.

My harmonization project allowed me to see presuppositional capitulation from the inside.

My IT, enterprise-architecture, systems-engineering, and modeling-and-simulation background taught me to inspect models, boundaries, assumptions, interfaces, initial conditions, verification, and validation.

My exemplars gave me increasingly precise philosophical and methodological tools.

Regulative supernaturalism reopened the causal vocabulary required by the biblical history while retaining explanatory accountability.

Designed Functional Maturity emerged as one research response to the initialization problem.

Methodological Designism began supplying governance for how such programmes should be developed and tested.

And the Biblical World Model became the architecture in which these commitments could be integrated without confusing any human model with God's world itself.

There is an irony in where the journey has brought me.

I now hold conclusions about creation that resemble important convictions with which I began. Yet I do not hold them in quite the same way.

The young creation of my childhood was substantially inherited. The position I hold now has passed through serious attempted harmonization, revision of my own systematic, presuppositional critique, systems analysis, model comparison, and continuing exposure to evidence that can place pressure on my preferred conclusions.

I reserve the right to be wrong.

If my exegesis fails, it should be corrected. If DFM accumulates unresolved explanatory burdens, those burdens should be recorded rather than hidden. If a Flood model fails against geological evidence, biblical authority does not rescue that particular geological model. If an auxiliary hypothesis exists mainly to protect a preferred conclusion, it should be recognized as ad hoc.

Christian confidence should be able to survive scrutiny.

That is why the question “Is there a third way?” was so important.

The answer was not another point on the line between young earth and old earth.

I was learning to ask a different class of question.

Who establishes the rules of inquiry? What counts as an observation? What has been inferred? Which assumptions connect the two? What causal history is admissible? Where are the initialization boundaries? Which claims are revelation, which are observations, which are models, and which remain hypotheses?

Those questions eventually led me toward a Biblical World Model.

The journey continues.
