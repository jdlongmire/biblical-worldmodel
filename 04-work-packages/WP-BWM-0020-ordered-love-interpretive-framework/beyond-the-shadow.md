## Abstract

Old Testament ethical challenges are often isolated from the canonical story that gives them their theological function. National judgment, warfare, slavery and servitude, severe penalties, ceremonial restrictions, and social accommodations are then treated as independent moral propositions. This paper proposes a canonical rubric grounded in ordered covenant love. Humanity is created for communion with God and neighbor under divine authority. Natural creaturely self-love is good within that communion and is uniquely presupposed rather than commanded. Creaturely autonomy disorders love by making the self the governing reference point of good. Rebellion brings the Curse and death. Divine law is present before the Fall and is later expanded and covenantally codified for fallen humanity. It can reveal, order, restrain, condemn, and teach, but it cannot regenerate an autonomous heart. The Old Testament therefore moves through communion, rebellion, Curse, law, judgment and accommodation, promise and preservation, toward the promised Seed, the redeemed Bride, and restored communion. This rubric clarifies difficult texts while leaving every individual passage accountable to its own exegesis.

## 1. The canonical problem

The Old Testament does not begin at Sinai. It begins with creation. Before Israel, Mosaic civil legislation, slavery, warfare, and national judgment stands a created order in which humanity receives being, vocation, relationship, moral boundaries, and delegated authority from God (Gen. 1:26-28; 2:15-17).

The governing sequence is therefore:

**Ordered covenant communion -> creaturely autonomy -> rebellion -> Curse and death -> external law confronting fallen humanity -> judgment, restraint, and accommodation -> promise and preservation -> the Seed -> regeneration and the Bride -> consummated covenant communion.**

Difficult Old Testament texts occur inside this history. They should be classified within it before conclusions are drawn about their moral function.

## 2. External law from the beginning

Divine law does not originate at Sinai. Adam receives explicit external command before the Fall (Gen. 2:16-17). Genesis 3 can describe transgression precisely because a creature stands under an objective divine prohibition.

Sinai therefore represents an expanded covenantal codification of divine law to a people already living in a fallen world. This distinction is decisive. The biblical problem is not the existence of external law. The decisive change is the human subject standing under it.

Before the Fall, external command addresses humanity in covenant communion. After the Fall, divine command addresses humanity whose loves have been disordered by attempted autonomy.

## 3. The Principle of Ordered Love

Jesus identifies love of God and love of neighbor as the commands upon which the Law and Prophets depend (Matt. 22:37-40; Deut. 6:5; Lev. 19:18). The formulation contains an anthropological asymmetry. God-love is commanded. Neighbor-love is commanded. Self-love is presupposed: the neighbor is to be loved "as yourself."

Paul makes the same assumption when he observes that a person ordinarily nourishes and cherishes his own flesh (Eph. 5:28-29). Scripture therefore does not require human beings to manufacture concern for their own good before they can obey the second great commandment.

### Principle of Ordered Love

**Human beings naturally possess creaturely self-love and therefore require no command to seek their own good. Within proper covenant communion, natural self-love is ordered under love for God and expressed toward the neighbor. Creaturely autonomy disorders this relation by making the self the governing reference point of good. Divine law commands love toward God and neighbor and establishes the boundaries within which natural self-love remains properly ordered.**

Self-love itself is not the Fall. Proper self-regard, preservation, nourishment, and creaturely good belong within created life. The disorder arises when the creature claims the authority to make the self the ultimate moral reference.

## 4. Autonomy and rebellion

Genesis 3 presents the temptation to be "like God, knowing good and evil" (Gen. 3:5). The creature receives God's judgment concerning good and evil and then asserts an alternative authority. Autonomy, in this theological sense, is the creature's attempted self-government at the level of ultimate moral determination.

The causal structure is:

**Creaturely autonomy -> self as governing reference -> disordered love -> transgression -> broken communion -> Curse and death.**

The consequences immediately move in both directions of commanded love. Adam and Eve hide from God (Gen. 3:8). Adam turns accusation toward the woman and indirectly toward God (Gen. 3:12). Cain kills Abel (Gen. 4:8). Rebellion against God and destruction of neighbor belong to the same disorder of covenant love.

## 5. Curse, death, and Mosaic law

The Curse precedes Moses. Paul states that death reigned from Adam to Moses (Rom. 5:12-14). Mosaic law therefore neither originates death nor creates the fallen condition it confronts.

The sequence is:

**Communion under divine authority -> transgression -> Curse and death -> increasingly articulated law confronting fallen humanity.**

Paul can consequently affirm that the law is holy and the commandment holy, righteous, and good (Rom. 7:12), while also describing sin as exploiting the commandment (Rom. 7:7-13). The defect lies in the rebellious human subject.

External law can reveal righteousness, identify transgression, restrain conduct, order covenant society, establish judicial boundaries, and teach typologically. It cannot regenerate the autonomous heart. The problem is deeper than ignorance of the good. Fallen humanity resists receiving the good from God as authoritative.

Israel's history consequently demonstrates that increasing specificity of external command does not itself restore covenant fidelity.

## 6. The Decalogue and autonomous self-love

The Decalogue can be read as an objective ordering of love. Its first commands govern humanity's relation to God. Idolatry, false worship, misuse of God's name, and rejection of divine authority are vertical expressions of autonomous self-reference.

The neighbor-directed commands restrain the horizontal consequences. Murder sacrifices the neighbor's life. Adultery violates the neighbor's covenantal union. Theft appropriates the neighbor's goods. False witness sacrifices the neighbor's good to another purpose. Covetousness exposes the inward desire beneath such appropriation.

The law constrains what autonomous self-love may do to Godward worship and neighborly relations. This explains why Christ can summarize the Law and Prophets through love of God and neighbor.

## 7. Interpretive taxonomy

Difficult texts should first be classified. At least six categories must remain distinct.

**Creation ordinance** concerns relationships and purposes belonging to God's good ordering of human existence and therefore supplies a normative creational horizon.

**Moral command** articulates obligations grounded in God's righteous order, centrally expressed through love of God and neighbor.

**Fallen-world accommodation and regulation** governs social realities already present within fallen civilization. Regulation does not logically entail that the regulated institution is a creation ordinance or eternal moral ideal.

**Judicial action** addresses rebellion and its consequences. Because human evil damages persons beyond the immediate actor, judgment can serve the defense of moral and covenantal order.

**Ceremonial and typological law** teaches through sacrifice, priesthood, purity, sacred space, and related patterns that anticipate fulfillment in Christ.

**Redemptive preservation** concerns God's historical preservation of the promise and covenant line through which the promised Seed comes.

These categories may overlap in a passage, but they cannot simply be collapsed into one another.

## 8. National judgment as a challenge case

Canaanite judgment must be read in its stated judicial context. Genesis 15:16 delays possession of the land because the iniquity of the Amorites is "not yet complete." The delay establishes patience and a moral threshold within the narrative. Deuteronomy 9:4-5 explicitly denies Israelite righteousness as the ground of possession and identifies the wickedness of the nations as part of the rationale.

The category is judicial rather than racial. Israel itself later receives severe judgment, invasion, destruction, and exile for covenant rebellion. Election does not grant moral immunity.

Ordered love also prevents love from being reduced to permissiveness. Autonomous self-love can become socially predatory. Violence, exploitation, murder, sexual abuse, oppression, and idolatrous social orders injure neighbors. A God who commands neighbor-love is not thereby prohibited from judging entrenched neighbor-destroying evil.

Redemptive preservation also belongs to the wider narrative. Israel's separation and survival serve the covenantal trajectory toward the promised Seed. This claim must remain proportional to individual texts. Scripture does not warrant treating every act of national judgment exclusively as a biological necessity for preserving the Messianic line.

The rubric therefore resolves the categorical objection that divine love and national judgment are inherently contradictory. It does not automatically establish the interpretation of every conquest text. Scope, target, rhetoric, historical setting, and judicial rationale still require passage-specific exegesis.

## 9. Slavery and servitude as a challenge case

Human dominion over other human beings is absent from the creation commission of Genesis 1-2. Humanity receives dominion over the nonhuman creation. Human hierarchy, coercion, and exploitation belong to the post-Fall social world.

Mosaic regulations concerning servitude should therefore be classified before they are treated as statements of the creational ideal. Ancient Israel contained multiple legally distinguishable forms of servitude, including debt service and service involving foreigners. Those differences must not be flattened.

The governing principle is:

**Divine regulation of a fallen social institution does not by itself constitute divine identification of that institution with the created moral ideal.**

Jesus provides a canonical precedent for this mode of reasoning in his treatment of divorce. Moses permitted a practice because of hardness of heart, while Jesus appealed beyond the Mosaic accommodation to the creation order: "from the beginning it was not so" (Matt. 19:3-9).

The Exodus simultaneously makes liberation from oppressive bondage part of Israel's defining memory. Hebrew debt-service receives release provisions and commands of generosity (Deut. 15:12-18). Man-stealing is a capital offense (Exod. 21:16). The New Testament later condemns `andrapodistai`, a term commonly rendered enslavers, slave traders, or kidnappers (1 Tim. 1:10). Lexical and institutional distinctions still require care.

These canonical controls prevent the inference that because Mosaic law regulates servitude, human ownership or exploitation is therefore the eternal moral telos. They also prevent an opposite simplification in which every ancient form of service is treated as identical to modern race-based chattel slavery. Each law must be examined on its own terms.

Within the ordered-love rubric, exploitative slavery is intelligible as autonomous self-love consuming the neighbor for the self's perceived benefit. Regulation restrains fallen conditions; regeneration addresses the deeper disorder.

## 10. Promise and preservation

The Fall is accompanied by promise. Genesis 3:15 introduces conflict between the serpent and the woman and between their offspring, with eventual victory over the serpent. From that point the canonical narrative repeatedly preserves the promise through human failure.

Noah survives judgment. Abraham is called. Isaac carries the covenant line. Jacob receives the promise. Judah is distinguished. David receives covenant promise. A remnant survives national apostasy and exile.

The Old Testament therefore narrates more than Israel's political history. It records the preservation of God's redemptive promise through the very rebellion that demonstrates humanity's inability to restore itself.

The controlling question increasingly becomes: **Where is the promised Seed, and how will God preserve His promise despite human rebellion?**

## 11. Christ and the resolution of ordered love

The framework reaches its center in Christ. Paul identifies Christ as the promised Abrahamic Seed (Gal. 3:16). Christ bears the Curse (Gal. 3:13), fulfills the Law (Matt. 5:17), obeys where Adam and Israel fail, and gives Himself for His Bride (Eph. 5:25-27).

His self-giving displays perfectly ordered love. He acts in obedience to the Father and for the good of His people. The biblical resolution therefore reaches deeper than cancellation of legal debt. Christ restores the covenant communion for which humanity was made and secures a people whose loves are reordered toward God and neighbor.

## 12. The New Covenant and internalized law

The solution to failed covenant fidelity is not the disappearance of divine law. Jeremiah promises that God will put His law within His people and write it on their hearts (Jer. 31:31-34). Ezekiel promises a new heart, a new spirit, and God's Spirit causing His people to walk in His statutes (Ezek. 36:25-27).

The solution corresponds to the problem. External command can expose and restrain rebellion. It cannot regenerate the rebel. The New Covenant addresses the heart from which disordered love proceeds.

Regeneration therefore restores the proper orientation of creaturely willing. Natural self-love is no longer to function as an autonomous moral terminus. The Spirit progressively orders the redeemed person's loves toward God and neighbor.

## 13. The Bride and consummated communion

The canonical story ends where its governing purpose began: God dwelling with His people. Revelation 21-22 depicts death abolished, the Curse removed, the tree of life accessible, God's servants before Him, and redeemed humanity participating in His kingdom.

The completed trajectory is:

**Communion -> autonomy -> rebellion -> Curse -> law and restraint -> judgment and accommodation -> promise -> preservation -> Seed -> redemption -> regeneration -> Bride -> consummated communion.**

The middle of the story is therefore interpreted by both its beginning and its end. Fallen institutions are not automatically creational ideals. Judicial interventions are not automatically violations of love. External law is neither evil nor salvific. It reveals and orders the good while exposing the autonomous heart that cannot restore itself.

## 14. Method for difficult texts

The rubric can be operationalized through a sequence of questions:

1. **Creation:** What creational good or relationship provides the normative horizon for this subject?
2. **Fall:** What effects of autonomy, Curse, death, hardness of heart, or social disorder are already present?
3. **Category:** Is the passage principally moral command, accommodation/regulation, judicial action, ceremonial typology, redemptive preservation, or some combination?
4. **Immediate exegesis:** What does the text actually command, permit, prohibit, judge, or symbolize? Who is addressed, under what covenantal conditions, and with what stated rationale?
5. **Canonical control:** How do earlier and later Scriptures constrain the interpretation without erasing the immediate context?
6. **Redemptive function:** How does the passage relate, if at all, to the preservation of promise and the movement toward Christ?
7. **New Covenant resolution:** Does the New Testament retain, transform, fulfill, internalize, or terminate the relevant covenantal form?
8. **Consummation:** What does the final biblical vision reveal about the enduring moral telos?

This method prevents two opposite errors. One treats every Mosaic regulation as an Edenic ideal. The other assumes that any difference between Mosaic regulation and New Covenant life demonstrates a change in God's moral character. Canonical categories permit continuity of moral order alongside different administrations within redemptive history.

## 15. Limits and falsification conditions

A useful framework must remain corrigible. This rubric should be rejected or revised where its claims cannot survive Scripture.

First, the claim that external divine law exists before Sinai depends directly on Genesis 2-3 and is therefore strongly grounded. Second, the claim that self-love is naturally presupposed rather than positively commanded rests principally on Leviticus 19:18, Matthew 22:39, and Ephesians 5:28-29. If those texts require a materially different anthropology, the Principle of Ordered Love must be revised.

Third, the accommodation category cannot be invoked merely because a modern reader dislikes a command. Its use requires textual or canonical warrant. Matthew 19 supplies an explicit example of Mosaic accommodation, but it does not prove that every difficult Mosaic institution belongs to the same category in the same way.

Fourth, national judgment must be justified from the stated judicial logic of the relevant texts. The framework cannot turn unstated motives into revealed facts.

Fifth, slavery and servitude require lexical, legal, historical, and covenantal distinctions. The framework does not establish that every form of servitude was equivalent, nor that regulation itself proves moral approval.

Finally, the canonical metanarrative cannot substitute for grammatical and historical exegesis. It constrains interpretation because Scripture interprets Scripture, but a controlling framework becomes illegitimate if it is used to silence contrary textual evidence.

## 16. Conclusion

The Old Testament's difficult texts belong to a coherent redemptive history. Humanity is created for loving covenant communion under divine authority. Natural creaturely self-love is good within that communion and requires no separate command. Love of God and neighbor are commanded because creaturely autonomy can turn natural self-concern into the governing reference point of good.

The Fall disorders love. The Curse and death follow. Divine law, already present before the Fall, is progressively articulated and covenantally codified in a world of autonomous fallen creatures. It reveals the good, restrains evil, orders covenant life, exposes transgression, and teaches redemptively. It cannot regenerate the heart.

This distinction supplies the controlling rubric for the Old Testament's ethical challenges. Judgment addresses mature rebellion and its destruction of covenantal and neighborly order. Accommodation regulates fallen social realities without necessarily identifying them as creation ideals. Ceremonial structures teach through shadow. Redemptive preservation carries the promise forward. The Seed comes in Christ, bears the Curse, fulfills the Law, gives Himself for the Bride, and secures the Spirit who writes God's law upon the heart.
