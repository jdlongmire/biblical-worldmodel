## Abstract

The ethical challenges of the Old Testament are frequently approached as isolated problems: national judgment, warfare, slavery and servitude, severe legal penalties, ceremonial restrictions, and divine accommodation to ancient social structures. Such treatment can obscure the canonical narrative within which these texts function. This paper proposes an interpretive framework grounded in rightly ordered covenant love. Humanity is created for communion with God and neighbor under divine authority. Creaturely self-love is natural and good within this communion and, uniquely among the principal directions of human love, does not require positive command. Creaturely autonomy disorders this structure by making the self the governing reference point for good. Rebellion follows, bringing alienation, Curse, and death. Divine law, present from the beginning and progressively articulated within redemptive history, objectively orders love and constrains the destructive consequences of autonomous self-love, yet external law cannot regenerate the rebellious heart. The Old Testament therefore unfolds through creation and communion, rebellion and Curse, law and restraint, judgment and accommodation, promise and preservation, culminating in the promised Seed through whom the Curse is borne, the law fulfilled, the Bride redeemed, and covenant communion restored. This framework supplies a coherent rubric for interpreting difficult Old Testament texts while requiring each individual passage to survive its own exegetical analysis.

## 1. Introduction

The Old Testament presents genuine interpretive difficulties. Israel receives commands concerning warfare, servitude, capital punishment, ritual purity, national separation, inheritance, marriage, property, sacrifice, and civil order. Entire nations fall under divine judgment. Institutions that appear morally objectionable to modern readers are regulated rather than immediately abolished.

These texts are often extracted from their canonical setting and treated as independent propositions about God's moral character. The resulting question is straightforward: if God is good, why does God command, permit, or regulate this?

The question is legitimate. Its formulation, however, can obscure a prior interpretive problem: what kind of world does the biblical narrative say these commands govern?

The Old Testament does not begin at Sinai. It begins with creation. Before Israel, Moses, civil legislation, slavery, warfare, and national judgment stands a created order in which humanity exists in covenant communion with God. The subsequent legal and historical material must therefore be interpreted against both what preceded it and what Scripture says follows it.

The canonical trajectory can be summarized:

**Ordered covenant communion -> creaturely autonomy -> rebellion -> Curse and death -> external law confronting fallen humanity -> judgment, restraint, and accommodation -> promise and preservation -> the Seed -> regeneration and the Bride -> consummated covenant communion.**

This trajectory supplies the interpretive rubric developed below.

## 2. The Created Order: Communion Under Divine Authority

Humanity begins neither autonomous nor morally self-defining. Genesis presents human beings as creatures whose existence, vocation, environment, relationships, and moral boundaries are received from God. Humanity bears God's image and exercises delegated dominion over creation (Gen. 1:26-28). Adam is placed within the garden and commissioned to work and keep it (Gen. 2:15).

Divine law is therefore present before the Fall. God commands Adam concerning the trees of the garden and explicitly prohibits eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Gen. 2:16-17). The existence of transgression in Genesis 3 presupposes an objective command capable of being transgressed.

Sinai does not introduce external divine law. Sinai represents a later and vastly expanded covenantal articulation of divine law to fallen humanity.

The biblical problem therefore cannot be that external law itself produces human rebellion, because humanity receives external command before the Fall. The decisive change occurs in humanity's relation to divine authority. The creature moves from communion under God's authority to attempted autonomy.

## 3. The Principle of Ordered Love

Christ identifies the fundamental moral structure of Scripture in two commands: love God with the whole person and love the neighbor as oneself (Matt. 22:37-40; cf. Deut. 6:5; Lev. 19:18). He states that all the Law and the Prophets depend upon these commands.

The formulation contains an important anthropological asymmetry. Human beings are commanded to love God. Human beings are commanded to love their neighbors. They are not commanded to love themselves. Neighbor-love instead presupposes self-love: "as yourself."

Paul employs the same assumption when discussing marriage. A man who loves his wife loves himself, and Paul grounds the analogy in the ordinary human practice of nourishing and cherishing one's own flesh (Eph. 5:28-29).

Creaturely self-love, properly understood, is therefore neither inherently sinful nor an unfortunate consequence of the Fall. Human beings naturally seek their own preservation and good. Within proper covenant communion, such self-love is good and orderly. The relevant moral question is how natural self-love is ordered.

### Principle of Ordered Love

**Human beings naturally possess creaturely self-love and therefore require no command to seek their own good. Within proper covenant communion, natural self-love is ordered under love for God and expressed analogically toward the neighbor. Creaturely autonomy disorders this relation by making the self the governing reference point of good. Divine law consequently commands human love outward toward God and neighbor and establishes the boundaries within which natural self-love remains properly ordered.**

This principle does not treat self-care, preservation, or legitimate creaturely good as vice. It identifies autonomy as the disordering move by which a derivative creature claims moral ultimacy for the self.

## 4. Creaturely Autonomy and the Fall

Genesis 3 concerns more than violation of an isolated dietary prohibition. The serpent offers the possibility of creaturely moral autonomy: "you will be like God, knowing good and evil" (Gen. 3:5). The temptation concerns authority over the determination of good and evil. Humanity receives God's declaration concerning reality and then chooses another epistemic and moral authority.

The creature attempts to become the governing reference point.

The resulting sequence is:

**Creaturely autonomy -> self-reference as governing authority -> disordering of love -> transgression of divine law -> rupture of covenant communion -> Curse and death.**

Self-love itself does not cause the Fall. Self-love detached from its proper covenantal ordering becomes destructive because the creature's perceived good can now be pursued against God and against neighbor.

The immediate consequences are revealing. Adam and Eve hide from God (Gen. 3:8). Adam turns against the woman and indirectly against God (Gen. 3:12). Cain subsequently kills Abel (Gen. 4:8). The vertical rupture between humanity and God becomes horizontal rupture between human beings. Autonomy disorders both directions of commanded love.

## 5. The Curse and the Reign of Death

The Curse precedes Sinai. Paul explicitly states that death reigned from Adam to Moses (Rom. 5:12-14). The Mosaic law therefore cannot be the origin of either sin or death. Humanity already exists under the reign of death when the Mosaic covenant is established.

The sequence is therefore:

**Communion under divine authority -> transgression -> Curse and death -> increasingly articulated law confronting fallen humanity.**

The Law enters a world already broken. This distinction preserves Paul's insistence that the law itself is holy and the commandment holy, righteous, and good (Rom. 7:12). The defect resides in fallen humanity.

## 6. External Law and the Autonomous Heart

External law performs indispensable functions. It identifies good and evil, establishes boundaries, restrains certain destructive behavior, orders covenant society, exposes transgression, reveals divine holiness, establishes judicial categories, and supplies ceremonial and typological patterns that anticipate redemption.

What external law cannot do is regenerate an autonomous heart.

Romans 7 expresses the problem sharply. Sin exploits the commandment itself as an occasion for rebellion (Rom. 7:7-13). The problem is therefore deeper than informational deficiency. Humanity does not merely need to know what is good. Fallen humanity resists receiving the good from God as authoritative.

Increasing legal specificity cannot finally solve that problem. The autonomous heart encounters the divine "You shall" while asserting its own "I will." The Law remains good. The rebellious subject remains the problem.

The history of Israel consequently becomes, among other things, a historical demonstration of the insufficiency of external law to restore covenant communion.

## 7. The Decalogue as the Ordering of Love

The principle of ordered love illuminates the structure of the Decalogue. The first commandments govern humanity's relation to God. Fallen self-reference produces idolatry, manipulative worship, misuse of God's name, and rejection of divine authority.

The remaining commandments govern humanity's relation to neighbor. Murder sacrifices the neighbor's life. Adultery violates the neighbor's covenantal union. Theft appropriates the neighbor's property. False witness sacrifices the neighbor's good for another purpose. Covetousness exposes the internal desire to appropriate what belongs to the neighbor.

These prohibitions establish boundaries upon what autonomous self-love may do in pursuit of its perceived good. The Law therefore does more than provide a catalogue of forbidden actions. It objectively orders creaturely love. This explains why Christ can summarize the entire Law and Prophets through love of God and neighbor.

## 8. A Taxonomy for Difficult Old Testament Texts

The framework requires distinctions among several kinds of biblical material. Failure to distinguish them produces apparent ethical contradictions.

### 8.1 Creation ordinance

Creation ordinances concern relationships and purposes belonging to God's good ordering of human existence prior to the Fall. They establish the normative creational horizon against which later accommodations should be evaluated.

### 8.2 Moral command

Moral commands articulate obligations grounded in God's righteous character and the proper ordering of creaturely relations. Their center is love of God and neighbor.

### 8.3 Fallen-world accommodation and regulation

Some laws regulate social realities arising within fallen human civilization. Regulation does not logically entail that the regulated institution constitutes God's creational ideal. A law governing an existing practice may restrict its operation, protect persons within it, impose liability, or prevent worse abuses without declaring the institution itself part of the original created order.

### 8.4 Judicial action

Divine judgment addresses rebellion and its consequences. Because autonomous human action can destroy neighbors, families, communities, and nations, divine love cannot be reduced to nonintervention. Judgment may protect moral and covenantal order against mature and destructive rebellion.

### 8.5 Ceremonial and typological law

Sacrifice, priesthood, ritual purity, sacred space, and related institutions possess pedagogical and anticipatory functions within redemptive history. They point beyond themselves toward the resolution accomplished in Christ.

### 8.6 Redemptive preservation

From Genesis 3:15 onward, Scripture follows a promised redemptive line. Noah is preserved. Abraham is called. Isaac is distinguished from Ishmael. Jacob carries the promise. Judah receives particular significance. David receives covenant promise. A remnant survives judgment and exile. These events collectively establish a trajectory toward the promised Seed.

## 9. National Judgment

National judgment represents one of the most difficult categories in the Old Testament. The conquest of Canaan cannot responsibly be reduced either to ordinary ancient warfare or to an unexplained assertion of divine prerogative.

Genesis establishes a judicial context. God tells Abraham that his descendants will not immediately possess the land because the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete (Gen. 15:16). The delay matters. Judgment is neither immediate nor presented as ethnically arbitrary. A period of patience precedes a later judicial threshold.

Deuteronomy explicitly denies that Israel receives the land because of its own righteousness and identifies the wickedness of the nations as part of the judicial rationale (Deut. 9:4-5). The category is therefore judicial rather than racial.

This becomes unmistakable when Israel itself subsequently receives severe covenant judgment. Israel cannot claim ethnic immunity from the moral order it was commissioned to represent. The prophetic literature repeatedly interprets invasion, destruction, and exile as judgment upon Israel's own rebellion.

The framework therefore reads national judgment through divine patience, judicial response to entrenched rebellion, protection of covenantal and neighborly order, and preservation of the redemptive trajectory. Care is required concerning the final category. Scripture connects Israel's separation and preservation with covenant faithfulness and the promised lineage. It does not follow that every individual act of judgment should be explained exclusively as necessary to prevent biological interruption of the Messianic line. The claim must remain proportional to the text.

The rubric therefore resolves the alleged contradiction between divine love and divine judgment at the categorical level. Love does not entail moral indifference toward the destruction of neighbors. A God who commands neighbor-love may judge entrenched social evil precisely because human autonomy has consequences for persons beyond the autonomous actor. The legitimacy, scope, and historical function of a particular judgment must still be established exegetically.

## 10. Slavery, Servitude, and Fallen Social Structures

The same rubric approaches slavery and servitude through a different category. Human mastery over other human beings does not appear as a creation ordinance in Genesis 1-2. Humanity collectively receives dominion over the nonhuman creation. No human being is given creational dominion over another image-bearer.

Hierarchical domination appears within the world of the Fall. Consequently, Mosaic laws regulating forms of servitude must first be classified before they are treated as declarations of an eternal moral ideal.

Ancient Israel contained legally distinguishable forms of servitude, including debt-related service and forms involving foreigners. Individual texts require individual analysis. The broad interpretive principle nevertheless remains:

**Divine regulation of a fallen social institution does not by itself constitute divine identification of that institution with the created moral ideal.**

This principle is recognizable within Scripture's treatment of other institutions. Jesus reasons similarly regarding divorce. Moses permitted divorce because of hardness of heart, yet Christ directs the question back beyond Mosaic accommodation to creation: "from the beginning it was not so" (Matt. 19:3-9).

That establishes an important canonical hermeneutical precedent. A Mosaic provision can regulate conduct within conditions produced by hardness of heart while the creational order remains the higher normative reference.

The relevant question concerning servitude therefore becomes: what does this particular law accomplish within a fallen social structure, and how does that regulation relate to the wider canonical trajectory of love of neighbor, human dignity, liberation, and redemption?

The answer must be established text by text. The framework prevents the category error of assuming that regulation equals creational endorsement.

The Exodus itself also establishes liberation from oppressive bondage as a defining feature of Israel's corporate memory (Exod. 1-20). Hebrew debt-service receives release provisions and commands concerning generosity (Deut. 15:12-18). Man-stealing is treated as a capital offense (Exod. 21:16), and the New Testament later places enslavers or slave traders among conduct contrary to sound doctrine (1 Tim. 1:10, translation-dependent lexical nuance acknowledged). These texts do not erase the difficult Mosaic servitude texts. They establish canonical controls against reading human commodification as the creational telos.

## 11. Promise and Preservation of the Seed

The Fall does not terminate God's covenantal purpose. Judgment in Genesis 3 is accompanied by promise. Genesis 3:15 introduces the expectation of conflict between the serpent and the woman and between their offspring, culminating in victory over the serpent.

From this point onward, biblical history possesses a redemptive trajectory. Human rebellion repeatedly threatens destruction. Cain kills Abel. Violence fills the earth before the Flood. Human pride reappears at Babel. The patriarchal family repeatedly fails. Israel rebels in the wilderness. The judges cycle through apostasy. The monarchy fractures. The northern kingdom falls. Judah goes into exile.

Yet the promise survives.

The Old Testament therefore tells more than the history of Israel. It narrates the preservation of the promise through history. The question beneath much of the narrative becomes: **Where is the promised Seed, and how will God preserve His covenant promise despite persistent human rebellion?**

The answer progressively narrows toward the Messiah.

## 12. Christ as the Resolution

The framework reaches its center in Christ. Paul identifies Christ with the promised Abrahamic Seed (Gal. 3:16). Where Adam rebels, Christ obeys. Where Israel fails, Christ fulfills. Where external law exposes transgression, Christ fulfills its righteous demand. Where humanity stands beneath the Curse, Christ becomes a curse for His people (Gal. 3:13).

Where autonomous self-love sacrifices the neighbor for the self, Christ gives Himself in obedience to the Father for the good of His Bride (Eph. 5:25-27; Phil. 2:5-11).

His obedience is the perfect expression of rightly ordered love.

The resolution is therefore covenantal, moral, and redemptive at once. Christ does not merely cancel a legal deficit. He restores the communion for which humanity was created and constitutes a redeemed people whose loves are progressively reordered toward God and neighbor.

## 13. The New Covenant: Law Written Within

The resolution of the problem is not the disappearance of divine law. Jeremiah describes the New Covenant in terms of God's law placed within His people and written upon their hearts (Jer. 31:31-34). Ezekiel supplies the complementary promise of a new heart, a new spirit, and God's Spirit causing His people to walk in His statutes (Ezek. 36:25-27).

The location of the problem and the character of the solution correspond.

External command can identify and constrain rebellion. It cannot regenerate the rebel. The New Covenant therefore addresses the human heart.

God's moral order is internalized through regeneration and the work of the Spirit. The redeemed creature increasingly desires what accords with God's righteous order because the underlying orientation of the person is being restored.
