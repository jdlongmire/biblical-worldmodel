## 7. The Monterey Formation: An Independent Test Case

The Monterey Formation provides a second major example because it differs from the Barnett in age, depositional setting, lithology, tectonic history, and petroleum-system architecture. If the timing problem were peculiar to one shale play, it would have limited significance. The Monterey shows why the question must be asked more broadly.

The Miocene Monterey is one of the most important petroleum source systems in California. In the Santa Maria province, USGS work identifies Monterey organic-rich lithologies as the dominant source of petroleum and also recognizes Monterey intervals as important reservoirs. The formation is heterogeneous. It includes siliceous, phosphatic, carbonate-rich, and organic-rich facies, together with fractured chert and porous diatomaceous intervals. That heterogeneity matters because the word `shale` can obscure a complex rock system whose mechanical and hydraulic properties vary greatly through space and time.

David Williams' notes emphasize two observations. First, the Monterey is a prolific source. Second, petroleum occurs both within Monterey reservoirs and in fields whose charge is attributed principally to Monterey source intervals. Those observations again force the interpreter to distinguish generation from retention and expulsion.

The exact magnitude of Monterey petroleum resources must be handled carefully. Historical literature contains very large estimates of oil originally present in Monterey-related rocks, including estimates exceeding 500 billion barrels, but such figures are not equivalent to proved reserves or technically recoverable resources. They depend strongly on geographic area, stratigraphic interval, the definition of oil in place, and methodology. The book does not need a spectacular resource number to make the geological point. The essential fact is the scale and regional importance of Monterey petroleum generation.

The Monterey also demonstrates why migration cannot be reduced to matrix permeability alone. Fracturing has long been central to production from many Monterey reservoirs. Tectonic deformation, brittle siliceous lithologies, pressure history, and fracture connectivity all affect fluid movement. A migration model that ignored those features would be incomplete. Yet a fracture-based explanation remains historical. Fractures must exist, be connected, and be transmissive when petroleum is available to move. A fracture that formed after the main charge interval cannot transport that earlier charge unless remigration occurs. A fracture that mineralized before generation cannot serve as the required pathway. Mechanism and timing are inseparable.

This makes the Monterey valuable as a methodological counterweight to the Barnett. The Barnett highlights extreme tightness and the contrast between retained unconventional hydrocarbons and expelled conventional charge. The Monterey highlights lithologic heterogeneity and fracture-dominated behavior. If both systems preserve evidence that migration was constrained by an earlier hydraulic state, the inference is stronger than either case alone. If they require different histories, that result is equally important. A useful theory must permit different rocks to behave differently.

## 8. Compaction Is a Process, Not a Number

The word `compaction` can create false simplicity. Sediment does not pass instantaneously from uncompacted to compacted. Compaction is progressive and includes several mechanisms.

Immediately after deposition, fine-grained sediment may contain very high porosity and large volumes of pore water. Increasing overburden rearranges grains, collapses larger pores, deforms ductile particles, and expels water. With greater burial, mechanical compaction is increasingly supplemented by chemical processes. Pressure solution, cement precipitation, mineral transformations, organic maturation, and changes in effective stress modify the pore system. The permeability decline can be much more dramatic than the porosity decline because flow depends on connected pore throats rather than total pore volume alone.

This distinction is central to source-rock migration. A shale can retain appreciable porosity while possessing extremely low permeability because its pores are minute and poorly connected. Conversely, fractures or bedding-parallel partings can provide high-conductivity pathways through a matrix whose intrinsic permeability remains tiny. The effective hydraulic system is therefore a combination of matrix properties and larger-scale discontinuities.

Historical petroleum literature often linked primary migration to compaction and water expulsion. Barker (1978), for example, emphasized that water is abundant during early compaction and diminishes with depth while pore size decreases. He concluded that migration mechanisms likely change as burial proceeds rather than remaining constant. Aoyagi and Asakawa (1980) likewise developed a primary-migration model tied to diagenetic stages and concluded that oil migration chiefly occurred during a late-compaction interval in their framework. Other authors challenged water-driven models, particularly because substantial oil generation may occur after much mechanical compaction has already taken place. USGS reviews subsequently emphasized continuous-phase hydrocarbon expulsion and pressure-driven mechanisms as more plausible in many mature source rocks.

The disagreement is instructive. `Compaction expelled the oil` cannot be assumed as a universal mechanism. Nor can `oil generation occurs after compaction` be used as a universal rebuttal. Basin histories differ. Source rocks differ. Burial rates differ. Mineralogies differ. Organic matter differs. Overpressure differs. The temporal overlap among water loss, petroleum generation, fracture development, and carrier-bed permeability must be established for the system under study.

David's working thesis therefore needs a more precise formulation than the strongest wording in his original notes. The defensible proposition is that many source rocks and their surrounding carrier systems undergo major reductions in connected permeability during burial and diagenesis, and that petroleum expulsion and migration must be reconstructed relative to that evolving hydraulic architecture. Where field evidence places charge before substantial permeability reduction, early migration is indicated. Where generation-induced fracturing or later tectonism provides demonstrable pathways, later migration remains viable.

That formulation does not weaken the investigation. It tells us what evidence can discriminate among histories.

## 9. Diagenesis as a Geological Clock

The most promising evidence may come from relationships in which petroleum and diagenesis record their relative order.

A reservoir is not simply deposited and then left unchanged until petroleum arrives. Mineral cements precipitate. Grains dissolve. Compaction reduces pore volume. Pressure solution creates stylolites. Authigenic clays grow. Carbonates recrystallize. Silica is mobilized. These processes can improve or destroy reservoir quality. Petroleum entering the pore system may also alter subsequent diagenetic reactions by displacing water, changing chemical activity, coating mineral surfaces, or modifying the fluids available for cementation.

If a reservoir contains evidence that hydrocarbon saturation preceded a later diagenetic event, that relationship provides a relative age for charge. For example, a cement that occurs below an oil-water contact but is inhibited within the oil-saturated interval can potentially indicate that petroleum occupied the reservoir before that cementation episode. Likewise, preserved anomalous porosity in an oil-bearing zone relative to water-bearing equivalents may indicate that charge affected subsequent compaction or cementation. These relationships have been discussed in petroleum literature under several labels, including hydrocarbon inhibition of diagenesis and the `Füchtbauer effect`.

Wilson's compilations rely heavily on such examples. That makes them potentially important and potentially dangerous. A compilation can create an impression of overwhelming evidence even when individual cases have different causes or uncertain chronology. Each field must therefore be reconstructed from its original geological study wherever possible.

The required questions are straightforward. What diagenetic feature is being measured? Is its timing independently constrained? Is the contrast genuinely controlled by hydrocarbon saturation, or could facies, mineralogy, temperature, pressure, or fluid composition explain it? Is the present oil-water contact the same as the paleo-contact? Has the field tilted? Was petroleum introduced once or repeatedly? Are there fluid inclusions or geochemical markers that independently constrain charge timing?

Only after those questions are answered can a diagenetic relationship be used as a clock.

If a large number of independent fields survive that test, however, the result would be powerful. The evidence would no longer consist merely of calculations about whether oil could move through a shale. It would consist of the rocks themselves recording that petroleum was present before specific stages of their later alteration.

That is why the global diagenetic catalog will occupy a major part of this book.

## 10. Long-Distance Migration and the Carrier-System Problem

David's notes also compile examples of long-distance lateral migration and large vertical separation between proposed source rocks and reservoirs. Such cases raise a different question. Even after petroleum leaves the source, how does it cross the basin efficiently enough to create commercial accumulations?

Secondary migration is generally less controversial than primary expulsion. Once petroleum enters a sufficiently permeable, water-wet carrier system, buoyancy provides an important driving force. Capillary forces oppose entry into smaller pores, so petroleum preferentially follows coarser and better-connected portions of carrier beds. England (1994) emphasized that the resulting path can be tortuous and strongly controlled by heterogeneity. Hydrodynamic gradients can alter migration direction. Faults can focus or redirect flow. Multiple carrier levels may interact.

Distance alone therefore does not demonstrate rapid or early migration. Petroleum can travel substantial distances through favorable carrier systems. What matters for the present thesis is whether the carrier system available at the inferred time of charge differs materially from the system reconstructed at later stages.

Consider a source interval connected during early burial to a laterally extensive sandstone. Continued burial may cement portions of that sandstone, compartmentalize it with faults, or alter pressure gradients. Alternatively, later dissolution or fracturing may improve connectivity. A modern map of reservoir quality is therefore insufficient. The migration map must be a paleohydraulic map.

The same applies vertically. Petroleum found far above a source interval may have moved through faults, fractures, unconformities, carrier beds, salt-related structures, or combinations of these. The vertical distance is not itself the decisive evidence. The chronology of those pathways is.

This reframes the author's long-distance catalog. The purpose is not to argue that petroleum could never travel those distances under conventional petroleum physics. It plainly can. The purpose is to ask whether source correlation, structural history, diagenesis, and carrier architecture constrain when that travel occurred and whether those constraints repeatedly favor an early migration interval.
