## 1. A Petroleum Accumulation Is an Event in Time

A petroleum trap is usually drawn as a spatial problem. A source rock lies below or beside a carrier system. Hydrocarbons leave the source, move through permeable beds or fractures, encounter a reservoir, and accumulate beneath a seal. Anticlines, faults, stratigraphic pinch-outs, reefs, salt structures, unconformities, and combinations of these geometries dominate the diagrams because geometry is indispensable. Petroleum must have somewhere to go, a pathway by which to get there, pore space in which to accumulate, and a seal capable of retaining it.

Yet every element of that familiar diagram has a history.

The source rock was once uncompacted sediment. Its pore system changed as burial proceeded. Water was expelled. Mineral cements precipitated or dissolved. Organic matter matured. Hydrocarbons were generated. Pore pressure changed. Fractures opened, propagated, closed, or were mineralized. Carrier beds underwent their own compaction and diagenesis. Faults were created or reactivated. Structures grew. Seals became effective. Reservoir quality improved in some intervals and deteriorated in others. Petroleum itself changed composition as generation, migration, biodegradation, thermal cracking, leakage, and remigration proceeded.

A petroleum system therefore cannot be understood from its final geometry alone. It is a sequence of changing states.

That observation leads to the question at the center of this book: **When did the hydrocarbons move relative to compaction, diagenesis, structural development, and trap formation?**

The question sounds elementary. In practice, it reaches into one of the most difficult parts of petroleum geology. The movement of petroleum out of fine-grained source rocks has long been recognized as necessary for conventional accumulations, yet the physical mechanisms and efficiencies of primary migration or expulsion have remained subjects of substantial debate. Mackenzie et al. (1983) described compelling evidence for movement of petroleum from organic-rich shales into sandstones and limestones and identified compaction as one possible agent of expulsion during early maturity. Price (1994), more than a decade later, still described primary petroleum migration as one of the least understood parameters controlling the genesis of oil deposits. Modern work has added sophisticated models of hydrocarbon-generation overpressure, natural hydraulic fracturing, multiphase flow, transient microfractures, and fault-controlled migration, but the fundamental historical problem remains: a petroleum accumulation records processes that occurred in a rock system that may have been physically very different when the petroleum moved.

This distinction between the present rock and the rock at the time of migration is the starting point for the argument developed here.

The problem becomes particularly sharp in unconventional source-rock reservoirs. Horizontal drilling and multistage hydraulic fracturing have made commercial production possible from formations that earlier generations of petroleum geologists knew primarily as source rocks or seals. In these rocks, present-day matrix permeability can be extraordinarily low. Yet some of the same formations are geochemically demonstrated sources for petroleum accumulated in conventional reservoirs outside the source interval. The source rock therefore presents two facts at once: it can retain enormous quantities of hydrocarbons today, and it has expelled hydrocarbons in the past.

Those facts demand a history.

## 2. Source, Reservoir, Seal, and Pathway

The conventional petroleum-system vocabulary remains useful because it separates functions that are often confused. A **source rock** contains sufficient organic matter of appropriate type and maturity to generate petroleum. A **reservoir rock** contains connected pore space capable of storing and transmitting petroleum at economically meaningful scales. A **seal** inhibits petroleum movement sufficiently to permit accumulation. A **trap** is the geometric and stratigraphic configuration that allows petroleum to accumulate beneath or against a seal. A **carrier system** provides pathways through which expelled petroleum can migrate from source toward an accumulation.

A single formation may perform more than one of these functions. That fact is central to unconventional petroleum systems. The Barnett Shale, for example, is both a major source rock and a productive reservoir. In the broader Bend Arch–Fort Worth Basin petroleum system it is also the primary source for oil and gas occurring in Paleozoic reservoir rocks outside the Barnett itself (Pollastro et al., 2007; Hill et al., 2007). The Monterey Formation likewise functions as source and reservoir across important California petroleum systems. USGS work identifies the Monterey as the principal source and reservoir in the onshore and offshore Santa Maria basins (Isaacs, 1992).

When a source rock is also a reservoir, the usual source-to-reservoir diagram becomes less intuitive. Some generated petroleum remains in or near the source. Some may be expelled. Some may migrate into adjacent or distant reservoirs. Some may be lost. Some may be cracked from oil to gas. The relative amounts depend on organic richness, kerogen type, thermal history, pressure, saturation, rock fabric, fractures, carrier architecture, and the history of the surrounding basin.

This is why the term **migration** must be used carefully. Older petroleum literature commonly distinguishes primary migration, the movement or expulsion of petroleum from the fine-grained source bed into a more permeable carrier or reservoir, from secondary migration, the subsequent movement through the carrier-reservoir system. Different authors divide the process somewhat differently, especially when separating generation, expulsion from kerogen, movement through source-rock pore networks, and movement through transient fractures. The terminology matters, but the historical question survives the terminology: petroleum found outside a demonstrated source rock crossed a source-rock boundary and then traveled through some pathway to its present or former accumulation.

That movement requires a physically possible state of the system.

## 3. The Rock We Measure Is the End Product

A core taken from a producing formation today is a sample of the formation after its burial, compaction, cementation, dissolution, deformation, maturation, uplift, pressure evolution, and production history. Its measured permeability is real. It is also historical information from one point in the formation's history, usually the latest point.

It does not follow that the same permeability existed when petroleum was generated or expelled.

This principle is obvious in ordinary sedimentology. Newly deposited mud contains abundant water and high porosity. Burial reduces pore volume. Mechanical compaction rearranges grains and deforms ductile components. Chemical compaction and cementation further alter the pore network. Clay transformations modify mineralogy and water distribution. Organic maturation changes both solid organic matter and the volume and composition of generated fluids. The resulting shale or mudstone may bear little hydraulic resemblance to the sediment from which it developed.

The same principle applies to carrier beds and reservoirs. Sandstones may lose primary porosity through compaction and cementation, gain secondary porosity through dissolution, or undergo multiple diagenetic episodes. Carbonates may be dolomitized, dissolved, cemented, fractured, pressure-solved, or recrystallized. Fault zones may act as conduits at one stage and barriers at another. Fractures may temporarily increase permeability and later seal.

The migration problem is therefore intrinsically time-dependent.

One cannot simply measure a present permeability and insert that value unchanged into every earlier stage of the basin. Conversely, one cannot simply assume that an earlier rock was highly permeable enough to solve every migration problem. Both moves bypass the evidence. The historical permeability structure must be reconstructed from sedimentology, burial history, diagenesis, geochemistry, pressure history, structural timing, fracture history, and the petroleum accumulations themselves.

This book focuses on that reconstruction.

The working hypothesis is that a substantial portion of petroleum migration in many of the systems examined here occurred during comparatively early stages of burial and diagenetic development, while effective migration pathways were more open than they are today and while compaction-related fluid movement could contribute to transport. If so, the timing of petroleum generation and expulsion relative to permeability reduction becomes inseparable from the timing of trap formation.

That is a hypothesis to be tested, not a premise to be protected.

## 4. The Barnett Shale: The Problem in Concrete Form

The Mississippian Barnett Shale of north-central Texas provides an unusually useful place to begin because its petroleum-system relationships are well established.

Hill et al. (2007) used detailed biomarker and light-hydrocarbon geochemistry to identify the marine Barnett Shale as the primary petroleum source rock in the Fort Worth Basin, while allowing that other units such as the Smithwick Shale may be locally important. Pollastro et al. (2007) likewise describe the Barnett as the primary source rock for oil and gas produced from Paleozoic reservoirs in the Bend Arch–Fort Worth Basin. The source relationship is therefore not dependent merely on geographic association. It is supported by petroleum geochemistry.

This matters because petroleum occurs both within the Barnett and outside it.

The Barnett became famous as an unconventional gas reservoir precisely because production from such a tight rock required technological intervention. Long horizontal wellbores and hydraulic-fracture stimulation create or connect flow pathways over volumes of rock that would otherwise deliver hydrocarbons to a well too slowly for economic production. The engineering problem is a reminder of the geological problem. The hydrocarbons retained in the source rock can be difficult to move even over short distances under present reservoir conditions.

Yet the Barnett petroleum system records expulsion.

Jarvie et al. (2007) estimated a total hydrocarbon-generation potential equivalent to approximately 820 billion cubic feet per section for a representative 350-foot Barnett interval under their stated assumptions. They estimated that approximately 60 percent of that generation potential was expelled, with the remainder retained and available for later secondary cracking where thermal maturity was sufficient. The exact number is model-dependent and should not be converted into a universal constant for the Barnett. Its importance here is more basic: a major quantitative petroleum-system study concluded that a large fraction of generated petroleum left the source-rock system.

The same study also estimated a net porosity increase of approximately 4.3 to 4.6 percent associated with conversion of kerogen to hydrocarbons under the modeled conditions. Hydrocarbon generation therefore changes the rock-fluid system as it proceeds. It is not a passive event occurring inside an otherwise static pore network.

This gives us the first form of the timing problem:

1. The Barnett generated large quantities of petroleum.
2. A significant fraction was expelled according to published petroleum-system modeling.
3. Barnett-derived petroleum occurs in reservoir intervals outside the Barnett.
4. The Barnett today is sufficiently tight that commercial production from the formation itself depends heavily on stimulation.
5. Therefore, the physical state and pathways under which natural expulsion and migration occurred require historical explanation.

The fifth statement is deliberately modest. It does not prove that migration occurred only before substantial compaction. It does not prove that matrix permeability was the sole controlling variable. It does not exclude hydrocarbon-generation overpressure, microfracturing, faults, bedding-plane pathways, carrier beds, or episodic migration. It establishes the question that those mechanisms must answer.

### 4.1 A source that became a reservoir

The development of the Barnett as an unconventional play changed how petroleum geologists could observe a source rock. Before large-scale unconventional development, source rocks were often characterized primarily by geochemical measurements, maturation models, and their relationship to conventional accumulations. Horizontal drilling and stimulation turned the source interval itself into a producing reservoir and exposed the degree to which petroleum could remain stored in extremely fine pore systems.

That retention is important. A source rock does not simply generate petroleum and empty itself. Generation, retention, expulsion, adsorption, dissolution, cracking, and migration interact. Jarvie et al.'s Barnett model explicitly divides generated petroleum between expelled material and retained material. The retained fraction becomes part of the unconventional resource and, at sufficient maturity, feedstock for secondary gas generation.

Thus the conventional and unconventional petroleum systems are not separate stories. They are different outcomes of the same generation history.

The petroleum that remained tells us about storage and retention. The petroleum that left tells us about expulsion and migration. The conventional accumulations tell us that pathways and traps existed at relevant times. The present tight rock tells us what the source has become.

The task is to put those observations into temporal order.

### 4.2 What present permeability can and cannot tell us

It is tempting to formulate the Barnett problem as a simple contradiction: if the shale is nearly impermeable today, oil could never have escaped it. That formulation is too strong.

Petroleum geology contains several mechanisms by which hydrocarbons can leave low-permeability source rocks. Hydrocarbon generation can raise pore pressure because fluids occupy different volumes and have different physical properties than the kerogen from which they form. If pressure becomes sufficiently high, natural hydraulic fractures or microfractures can develop. Berg and Gangi (1999), for example, modeled primary migration in low-permeability source rocks through oil-generation-induced microfracturing. Modern reviews continue to treat hydrocarbon-generation overpressure and associated fracturing as important processes in shale source rocks. Migration can also exploit pre-existing fractures, faults, bedding-plane weaknesses, and adjacent carrier beds. Expulsion may be episodic rather than continuous.

These mechanisms prevent a simplistic inference from present matrix permeability to absolute historical immobility.

But they do not eliminate the timing problem. They make it more precise.

If fractures carried petroleum, when were they open? If overpressure drove expulsion, when did pressure exceed the relevant threshold? If bedding-plane partings served as pathways, what was the compaction state at that time? If faults focused migration, when were they transmissive relative to generation? If a permeable carrier bed received expelled petroleum, had that carrier retained sufficient effective permeability? If the trap was structural, had the structure already formed? If the seal was diagenetic, had it already become competent?

Every alternative mechanism has a clock attached to it.

## 5. Generation, Expulsion, Migration, Entrapment

A useful way to frame the entire problem is to separate five events or event families:

**Generation.** Organic matter reaches conditions under which petroleum is produced.

**Expulsion.** Some generated petroleum escapes retention within the source-rock system.

**Migration.** Expelled petroleum moves through fractures, faults, carrier beds, or other connected pathways.

**Trap availability.** A geometry capable of accumulating petroleum exists and is connected to the migration system.

**Preservation.** The accumulation survives later leakage, deformation, biodegradation, thermal alteration, remigration, or breach.

These events need not occur as clean sequential steps. Generation and expulsion can overlap for long intervals. Migration can occur in pulses. A growing structure can trap petroleum while deformation continues. A reservoir can receive multiple charges of different maturity. A trap can leak and later recharge. Petroleum can migrate from one accumulation to another. Nevertheless, the five-part framework forces the interpreter to ask the right historical questions.

A present-day accumulation demonstrates successful conjunction. Petroleum was generated. At least some escaped the source. A pathway connected source and reservoir. The trap was available when charge arrived. Enough petroleum was preserved to remain detectable or producible.

A dry structure is more ambiguous. It may never have been connected to a mature source. The source may have been inadequate. Migration may have bypassed it. The seal may have failed. The reservoir may have been destroyed by diagenesis. Petroleum may have accumulated and later leaked. Or the structure may simply have formed too late.

The last possibility is the one this book will examine closely.

## 6. The Trap-Timing Hypothesis

Suppose two structurally similar traps lie within the same broad petroleum province. Both contain reservoir-quality rock. Both have seals. Both appear capable of holding petroleum today. One is productive and the other barren.

Geometry alone may not explain the difference.

If the productive trap existed during the principal migration episode while the barren trap developed afterward, the contrast becomes historical rather than merely structural. The productive trap was available when petroleum passed through the system. The late trap was not.

This idea is familiar in petroleum-system analysis. Charge timing and trap formation are standard exploration concerns. The argument developed in this book presses the point further by asking whether a broad body of field evidence indicates that significant migration commonly occurred earlier in basin history than is sometimes assumed, particularly before compaction and diagenesis had reduced many primary pathways to their later condition.

The author's working notes assemble numerous proposed examples of this type. They include diagenetic traps in which porosity or permeability relationships appear to record hydrocarbon emplacement before later cementation; cases in which hydrocarbon presence appears to have inhibited compaction or diagenesis; isolated sand lenses and closed systems; long-distance lateral and vertical migration; and barren structures situated near productive structures but interpreted to have developed too late to receive charge.

Those examples will not be treated as proven merely because they appear in a catalog. Each must survive source verification and geological scrutiny. Some may prove strong. Some may require qualification. Some may fail. A global argument becomes stronger when weak examples are removed rather than defended.

The hypothesis can therefore be stated in falsifiable form:

> **Where petroleum migration occurred principally during an early interval of basin evolution, traps that became effective only after that migration interval should systematically lack the charge observed in otherwise comparable traps that were available earlier, unless later charge, remigration, or another petroleum system supplied them.**

This prediction has several implications. First, barren traps are potentially informative rather than merely disappointing. Second, diagenetic relationships may preserve temporal information about charge. Third, geochemical correlation between source and reservoir can constrain the existence of migration even when the original pathway is no longer permeable. Fourth, the distribution of petroleum should be analyzed against reconstructed paleostructure rather than modern structure alone.
