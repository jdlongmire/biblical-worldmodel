### I) continued — narrative (p.2–3)

Recent advances in the technology of horizontal drilling coupled with multistage hydraulic fracture stimulation has resulted in major oil and gas production increases in the United States. This technology is also being applied in other countries such as Canada, China and Argentina but on a much smaller scale. The wells are being drilled in what has become known as "unconventional shales." These shales are really a mix of shales (clay size particles), siltstones (silt size particles), sandstones (sand size particles) and carbonates. These formations have been drilled through by hundreds of thousands of wells in the past and were the large majority of the known and suspected source rocks that sourced the oil and gas of most of the conventional oil and gas production fields in these areas.

The pores in these source rocks are so small (1nm to 100nm, .001 to 0.1mm) and the flow paths are so tortuous that these rocks have permeabilities in the same (10–100 nanodarcy) range as most seals on overpressured reservoirs. [struck: "on unconventional/conventional reservoirs"] This is why average recoveries are so low — 5 to 10% in gas shales and 2 to 7% in oil shales — even with horizontal wells of 1 to 2 miles in length with multistage fracture treatments using in excess of 20 million pounds of sand.

This proves that these hydrocarbons had to originate in-situ in these source rocks and could not have migrated into these rocks. It also makes it highly unlikely that any primary migration of hydrocarbons out of these rocks has occurred [struck: "especially"] at current permeabilities. Migration had to take place prior to large-scale compaction and diagenesis taking place, when they had much larger permeabilities (100 to 1000 md or more). The only way this could occur is with very rapid rates of deposition that would allow the source rocks to reach the depths and temperatures required for major hydrocarbon generation prior to and during compaction.

Primary oil migration could then occur while large volumes of pore water was being expelled by the compaction process. The source rocks and surrounding formations would still have significant permeabilities that would allow both vertical and long-distance secondary migration into traps formed by the time of migration. Traps formed after migration occurred would therefore be barren of hydrocarbons. Many examples have been tested. See section VII.E.

### Mississippian Barnett Shale (p.4–6)

[marg, top of p.4: ") A) 2 & II) 1" — cross-reference to the Barnett entries in lists I)A and II) above/below]

The Mississippian Barnett Shale is an unconventional gas play in the Bend Arch – Fort Worth Basin of Texas. It includes parts of the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex area. Exploration and production of oil and gas began in the early 1900's. Prior to 1998 [marg: "and amounted to approximately 2 billion bbl oil and 7 TCF of gas"] the production was mostly from conventional reservoirs ranging in age from Ordovician to Permian. Detailed biomarker and light hydrocarbon geochemistry confirm that the marine Mississippian Barnett Shale is the primary source rock for petroleum in the Fort Worth Basin (Hill, Jarvie & others 2007). Gases in the Fort Worth Basin are thermogenic in origin and appear to be cogenerated with oils from the Barnett Shale, although some gas may also originate by oil cracking. The gases appear to be stratigraphically segregated — younger reservoirs contain less mature gas and older reservoirs contain more mature gas, except in the Pennsylvanian Bend Group (Hill, Jarvie, others 2007). This segregation of gases indicates multiple or continuous migration times. [marg *: "It is highly unlikely that this migration took place [struck: before] [red ink:] After compaction and diagenesis reduced the permeability of the [marginalia: left margin, handwritten with arrow: "Barnett Shale ⟶ B.S."] down to the current nanodarcy range."]

Oils derived from the Barnett from many different locations and with widely differing levels of maturity show highly similar biomarker signatures (Figure 5-5, *Petroleum Frontiers*) (Jarvie et al. 2001), indicating that fingerprinting has established the Barnett as the source for oils produced from the following intervals:

### Miocene Age — Monterey Shale (p.7)

[marg: ") A) 12)"]

The Miocene-Monterey Shale, long known as the critical source rock in California, also includes significant fractured chert & porous diatomite reservoirs (5). This formation has been estimated to contain more than 500 billion barrels of original oil-in-place (1).

Production from the Monterey Shale actually began in the Santa Maria Basin in 1900. The Monterey Formation is probably the principal source rock in the Santa Maria Basin as there are no other rocks with good source characteristics in the basin (4).

The Monterey Shale's potential as a source rock has outshone its potential as a reservoir rock for decades throughout the state of California, as companies began developing such mammoth fields as Midway-Sunset, South Belridge, Kern River, Cymric and Elk Hills. It is estimated that 38 billion barrels of oil has been produced from fields relying on the Monterey as source rock (2).
