## Direct framing

**The eternal call to loving covenant communion with God and neighbor.**

**The introduction and failure of law to constrain inherently rebellious humans and the reign of death under the Curse.**

**The promise and preservation of the Seed of Salvation for the Bride.**

The "introduction" of law in the second movement denotes its progressive formal articulation and covenantal codification within fallen human history. Divine external command itself is primordial and is already present in Eden (Gen. 2:16-17).

## Principle of Ordered Love

Human beings naturally possess creaturely self-love and therefore require no command to seek their own good. Within proper covenant communion, natural self-love is ordered under love for God and expressed toward the neighbor. Creaturely autonomy disorders this relation by making the self the governing reference point of good. Divine law commands love toward God and neighbor and establishes the boundaries within which natural self-love remains properly ordered (Deut. 6:5; Lev. 19:18; Matt. 22:37-40; Eph. 5:28-29).

Self-love is therefore not intrinsically rebellious. The disorder arises when the creature claims autonomy and makes the self the governing moral reference.

## Canonical trajectory

**Ordered covenant communion -> creaturely autonomy -> rebellion -> Curse and death -> external law confronting fallen humanity -> judgment, restraint, and accommodation -> promise and preservation -> the Seed -> redemption and regeneration -> the Bride -> consummated covenant communion.**

The Fall introduces the reign of death. Paul states that death "reigned from Adam to Moses" to establish that death already held humanity under its dominion before Mosaic codification (Rom. 5:12-14). Death continues through the Mosaic administration until its decisive defeat in Christ's death and resurrection, while biological death remains the last enemy awaiting final abolition at the consummation (Rom. 5:12-21; 1 Cor. 15:20-26, 54-57; Rev. 20:14; 21:4).

The redemptive chronology is therefore:

**Adam: death begins to reign -> Moses: Law enters an already death-ruled world -> Christ: death is decisively defeated -> consummation: death is finally abolished.**

## Function and limit of external law

Divine law is holy, righteous, and good (Rom. 7:12). It reveals righteousness, identifies transgression, restrains conduct, orders covenant society, establishes judicial boundaries, and teaches typologically. External law cannot regenerate the autonomous heart. Fallen humanity's problem includes resistance to receiving the good from God as authoritative (Rom. 7:7-13).

The Decalogue consequently orders love in both directions. Its God-directed commands constrain autonomous self-reference against God. Its neighbor-directed commands constrain what autonomous self-love may do to the neighbor. Murder, adultery, theft, false witness, and covetousness manifest different ways in which the perceived good of the self can consume or subordinate the good of another.

## Interpretive taxonomy

Difficult Old Testament texts shall be distinguished by category before moral conclusions are drawn:

- **Creation ordinance:** relationships and purposes belonging to God's good created order.
- **Moral command:** obligations grounded in God's righteous order, centrally expressed through love of God and neighbor.
- **Fallen-world accommodation and regulation:** governance of social realities present within fallen civilization. Regulation does not entail that the regulated institution is a creation ordinance or eternal moral ideal.
- **Judicial action:** divine response to rebellion and its destructive consequences.
- **Ceremonial and typological law:** anticipatory instruction through sacrifice, priesthood, purity, sacred space, and related forms.
- **Redemptive preservation:** God's historical preservation of the promise and covenant line through which the Seed comes.

These categories may overlap in a passage but shall not be collapsed into one another.

## National judgment

National judgment is interpreted judicially rather than racially. Genesis 15:16 establishes divine patience and a stated moral threshold before Amorite judgment. Deuteronomy 9:4-5 denies Israelite righteousness as the ground of possession and identifies the wickedness of the nations within the rationale. Israel's own later judgment and exile demonstrate that election grants no moral immunity.

Ordered love does not entail permissiveness toward entrenched neighbor-destroying evil. Autonomous self-love can become socially predatory through violence, exploitation, murder, sexual abuse, oppression, and other forms of destructive social order. Divine judgment can therefore function coherently within the same moral order that commands neighbor-love.

Redemptive preservation belongs to the wider covenantal trajectory toward the promised Seed. It shall not be used to infer that every individual act of national judgment was exclusively necessary to preserve the biological Messianic lineage. Each judgment text remains subject to passage-specific exegesis concerning scope, target, rhetoric, historical setting, and stated rationale.

## Slavery and servitude

Human dominion over other human beings is absent from the creation commission of Genesis 1-2. Mosaic regulations concerning servitude therefore require classification within fallen social conditions rather than automatic identification with the creational ideal.

**Divine regulation of a fallen social institution does not by itself constitute divine identification of that institution with the created moral ideal.**

Christ's treatment of divorce supplies a canonical precedent for distinguishing Mosaic regulation under hardness of heart from the creational telos (Matt. 19:3-9). This does not establish that every Mosaic regulation is accommodation of the same kind. It establishes the legitimacy of the distinction.

The Exodus establishes liberation from oppressive bondage within Israel's corporate memory. Hebrew debt-service receives release provisions and commands of generosity (Deut. 15:12-18). Man-stealing is prohibited under severe penalty (Exod. 21:16). First Timothy 1:10 condemns `andrapodistai`, with the precise English rendering requiring lexical care. Distinct ancient forms of service shall not be flattened into a single institution, and ancient servitude shall not be treated without analysis as identical to modern race-based chattel slavery.

Within the ordered-love rubric, exploitative slavery is a form of autonomous self-love consuming the neighbor for the self's perceived benefit. Regulation can constrain fallen conditions. Regeneration addresses the underlying disorder.

## Promise, Seed, and Bride

Genesis 3:15 introduces the redemptive promise within the judgment of the Fall. The subsequent canonical narrative preserves that promise through Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Judah, David, the remnant, exile, and restoration. The narrative increasingly asks how God will preserve His promise despite persistent human rebellion.

Christ is the promised Abrahamic Seed (Gal. 3:16). He bears the Curse (Gal. 3:13), fulfills the Law (Matt. 5:17), obeys where Adam and Israel fail, and gives Himself for His Bride (Eph. 5:25-27). His obedience and self-giving instantiate perfectly ordered love toward the Father and neighbor.

## New Covenant resolution

The New Covenant does not resolve the problem through disappearance of divine law. God promises to put His law within His people and write it upon their hearts (Jer. 31:31-34), to give a new heart and Spirit, and to cause His people to walk in His statutes (Ezek. 36:25-27).

External command exposes and constrains rebellion but cannot regenerate the rebel. The New Covenant addresses the heart from which disordered love proceeds. The Spirit restores creaturely willing toward its proper covenantal orientation under God and toward neighbor.

Revelation 21-22 completes the trajectory with God dwelling with His people, the Curse removed, death abolished, and the tree of life accessible.

## Interpretive method

Application of this framework requires asking:

1. What creational good supplies the normative horizon?
2. What effects of Fall and Curse are already present?
3. Which interpretive category or categories govern the passage?
4. What does the immediate text command, permit, prohibit, judge, or symbolize?
5. How does the wider canon constrain the reading?
6. What redemptive function is explicitly stated or reasonably warranted?
7. How does the New Covenant retain, transform, fulfill, internalize, or terminate the relevant covenantal form?
8. What does consummation reveal about the enduring moral telos?

The framework shall remain accountable to grammatical, historical, and canonical exegesis. Accommodation may not be invoked merely because a command presents a modern ethical difficulty. Judicial motives may not be invented where the text does not warrant them. Servitude requires lexical and institutional distinctions. Redemptive preservation may not substitute for passage-specific evidence.
