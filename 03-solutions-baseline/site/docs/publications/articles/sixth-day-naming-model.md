# The Naming of the Animals within the Sixth Day

## A Chronological Feasibility Model of Genesis 2:18–23

**James (JD) Longmire**  
ORCID: 0009-0009-1383-7698

## Abstract

A longstanding objection to reading the creation days of Genesis 1 as ordinary days holds that the events described in Genesis 2:7–25, especially Adam's naming of the animals, cannot fit within the sixth day. This paper tests that claim at the level actually required by the objection. It does not attempt to reconstruct the exact schedule of Day 6. It asks whether at least one coherent chronology exists that is compatible with the text and ordinary human capacities. Genesis 2:4–25 is treated as an elaboration of the human events associated with the sixth day; the pluperfect reading of Genesis 2:19 is grammatically defensible though contested; Genesis 2:20 restricts the explicitly named categories to livestock, birds, and beasts of the field; and published creationist Ark-kind estimates provide conservative bounds for a feasibility calculation. A working range of roughly 500–1,000 named kinds permits 18–36 seconds per kind in a five-hour model, while even the deliberately overinclusive Ark figure of 1,398 permits about 13 seconds per kind. The five-hour interval is a model parameter, not a textual datum. The argument from chronological impossibility therefore fails unless it can establish from the text that naming required substantially more time. The remaining dispute concerns an unstated duration, not a demonstrated contradiction.

## 1. The question and the burden of proof

Genesis 1 places the creation of man and woman on the sixth day. Genesis 2 describes the formation of the man, his placement in Eden, the divine command, the presentation and naming of animals, and the building and presentation of the woman. Old-earth creationist treatments have argued that too much occurs in Genesis 2 for these events to fit within an ordinary day (Campbell 2014).

The appropriate test is modest. If the objection is that the events **cannot** fit within an ordinary sixth day, a successful reply need establish only that a reasonable text-compatible chronology **can** fit. It need not establish the actual hour at which Adam was formed, the exact number of animals presented, the precise duration of naming, or the manner in which every animal approached him.

Accordingly, this paper distinguishes four epistemic categories throughout:

1. **Textual datum:** what Genesis states.
2. **Textual inference:** a conclusion argued from grammar, literary structure, or comparison of passages.
3. **Model parameter:** a value selected to test feasibility where Scripture supplies no value.
4. **Illustrative reconstruction:** a possible staging detail useful for visualization but carrying no exegetical authority.

Confusing these categories either overstates the young-earth case or makes the old-earth objection appear stronger than the text warrants.

## 2. Genesis 1 and Genesis 2

Genesis 2:4 opens with the first *toledot* formula of Genesis: "These are the generations of the heavens and the earth when they were created." The formula functions structurally within the book, and Genesis 2:4–25 narrows the focus from the cosmic account to the man, the garden, the woman, and their relation to God and the created order.

Collins (1995, pp. 138–139) argues that Genesis 2:4 forms a literary bridge between the accounts and supports reading 2:4–25 as an elaboration of the sixth day. Jesus likewise joins Genesis 1:27 and Genesis 2:24 in Matthew 19:4–5 when discussing the creation of male and female and the one-flesh union. Campbell (2014), although arguing for an old-earth interpretation, also treats Genesis 2:5–25 as a detailed treatment of the sixth day from the human perspective.

For the present argument, therefore, the principal question is duration rather than whether Genesis 1 and 2 concern unrelated human origins.

## 3. Genesis 2:19 and the order of animal formation

The ESV renders Genesis 2:19: "Now out of the ground the LORD God **had formed** every beast of the field and every bird of the heavens." The Hebrew verb is a *wayyiqtol*, ordinarily associated with sequential narrative. Whether it can carry a pluperfect sense here is disputed.

Collins (1995) argues that *wayyiqtol* can describe an event anterior to the preceding narrative under identifiable discourse conditions and treats Genesis 2:19 as a relevant case. Waltke and O'Connor likewise permit a pluperfect use in Hebrew syntax (1990, pp. 552–553, as cited in Kruger 2023). Leupold regarded a pluperfect translation of *yatsar* here as permissible (1942, p. 130, as cited in Lyons 2002). Hamilton acknowledged "had formed" as possible while preferring another harmonization (1990, p. 176, as cited in Lyons 2002).

The reading is not unanimous. Collins records Buth's objection to a harmonistic pluperfect interpretation. Cassuto instead proposed that the verse concerns particular specimens formed or prepared for presentation (1961, p. 129, as cited in Lyons 2002).

The proper conclusion is therefore limited: the pluperfect is **grammatically defensible**, not grammatically compelled. More importantly, Genesis 1 independently places birds on Day 5 and land animals earlier on Day 6 than humanity. The canonical day order already supplies the chronology that Genesis 2 elaborates.

## 4. Physical presentation and narrative function

Genesis 2:19 says that God "brought" the animals to the man. The same verbal form is used in 2:22 when God "brought" the woman to the man. Within a narrative involving ground, breath, garden, sleep, rib, flesh, animals, and woman, a physical presentation is the natural reading. A visionary presentation is unnecessary to the feasibility argument and is not assumed here.

The naming episode also has a narrative function beyond taxonomy. Genesis 2:18 first gives the divine assessment: "It is not good that the man should be alone." Genesis 2:20 then reports that among the creatures presented and named "there was not found a helper fit for him." Genesis 2:21–23 follows with the creation and presentation of the woman and Adam's recognition of her.

A useful interpretive synthesis is:

**God knows → God declares → Adam experiences → God provides → Adam recognizes.**

This sequence does not prove how long the naming lasted. It explains why the naming belongs naturally between the declaration of Adam's aloneness and the presentation of the woman.

## 5. Which animals does Genesis say Adam named?

Genesis 1:24–25 lists three broad categories of Day 6 land animals: livestock, creeping things, and beasts of the earth. Genesis 2:20 states that Adam named "all livestock and the birds of the heavens and every beast of the field." Sea creatures are absent. Creeping things are also absent from the explicit list.

Sarfati makes the same textual observation (2011, pp. 89–90, as cited in Campbell 2014). Leviticus 11:29–30 places several small ground animals within the category of swarming or creeping things, supporting the possibility that many small reptiles and similar animals fall outside the Genesis 2:20 list. The semantic boundaries of the Hebrew categories are broad, however, and should not be forced into modern Linnaean ranks.

The important point is methodological. The chronology should be tested against the categories Genesis actually names rather than against every animal species known today.

## 6. Kinds, estimates, and uncertainty

### 6.1 Published Ark-kind work

Creationist researchers have attempted to estimate the number of land-dwelling, air-breathing kinds represented on Noah's Ark. Lightner et al. (2011) describe a programme using hybridization data, the *cognitum*, and statistical baraminology. Published estimates include approximately 196 extant bird kinds (Lightner 2013), up to 300 mammal kinds including fossil forms (Lightner 2012), 101 extant reptile kinds across the cited studies, and 248 amphibian kinds across the cited studies. Ark Encounter reports a combined total of 1,398 living and extinct land-animal and flying-creature kinds (Ark Encounter n.d.).

These are **Ark-kind estimates**, not a direct count of the animals named in Genesis 2:20.

### 6.2 A working naming range

The Ark total is deliberately overinclusive for Genesis 2:20 because it incorporates creatures outside the explicit naming categories, including groups plausibly associated with creeping things. Conversely, fossil bird kinds and uncertainty about the relationship between created kinds and later Ark kinds prevent a precise subtraction.

No published study cited here directly computes a Genesis 2:20 naming total. Therefore **500–1,000 kinds is retained only as a working model range**, not as a biblical or taxonomic result.

The argument does not require confidence in that range. The full 1,398 Ark figure can be used as a conservative sensitivity bound. If the chronology remains feasible under a count known to overstate the relevant set, disputes over individual baramins have little leverage on the conclusion.

### 6.3 Dinosaurs

The earlier model proposed grouping crocodilians and non-avian dinosaurs into a single created kind. That commitment is unnecessary and has been removed from the feasibility argument.

Published creationist baraminology itself contains multiple proposed dinosaur groupings. Ross (2014b) concluded that broad dinosaur taxa are polybaraminic, and Doran et al. (2018) estimated 27 potential holobaramins among the dinosaur groups they examined. These results need not be adjudicated here. Whether dinosaurs represent one, several, dozens, or some other number of created kinds changes the timing calculation only marginally relative to the conservative upper bound.

The model therefore remains agnostic on the precise number of dinosaur kinds.

## 7. Timing model

### 7.1 Parameters

Genesis gives no clock schedule for the events. The following values are therefore **model parameters**:

- approximately five hours allocated to naming;
- a working named set of 500–1,000 kinds;
- the full 1,398 Ark-kind figure used as an intentionally conservative sensitivity case.

The five-hour value is selected because it allows substantially more observation per kind than rapid-fire labeling while leaving time within an ordinary day for the other narrated events. It is not claimed as the historical duration.

### 7.2 Arithmetic

Five hours contains 18,000 seconds.

| Kinds presented | Time per kind |
|---:|---:|
| 1,398 | about 13 seconds |
| 1,000 | 18 seconds |
| 750 | 24 seconds |
| 500 | 36 seconds |

At the working range, Adam would have approximately 18–36 seconds per kind. Even the overinclusive Ark bound allows about 13 seconds. Sarfati's much faster illustrative rate of five seconds per kind would complete 1,398 kinds in under two hours (2011, pp. 89–90, as cited in Campbell 2014), though the present model does not require so compressed a rate.

These calculations prove no historical duration. They establish that the number of creatures alone does not generate a chronological impossibility.

### 7.3 A feasible sequence

One possible sequence is:

1. The man is formed and given life (Genesis 2:7).
2. He is placed in the garden and receives vocation and prohibition (2:8, 15–17).
3. God declares that the man's solitude is not good (2:18).
4. Animals are brought to him and named (2:19–20).
5. The man sleeps; the woman is built and brought to him (2:21–22).
6. Adam recognizes the woman as corresponding to him (2:23), followed by the narrator's marriage statement (2:24–25).
7. Genesis 1:28–30 records the blessing and joint human commission.
8. Genesis 1:31 closes Day 6 with God's assessment of the completed creation as "very good."

The ordering of the Genesis 1:28 commission after the woman's creation follows its plural address. This is a reasonable canonical synthesis rather than an hour-by-hour transcript.

## 8. Objections

### 8.1 "This at last" requires a long interval

The ESV renders Adam's *happa'am* in Genesis 2:23 as "this at last." Campbell (2014) argues that the expression implies a prolonged search and cites lexical and narrative parallels. Sarfati prefers "this time" (2011, p. 90, as cited in Campbell 2014).

The expression clearly marks culmination. Its duration, however, comes from context. Genesis 46:30 uses comparable language after years of separation; other contexts involve much shorter periods. The lexical item does not itself encode a minimum number of days, months, or years.

Genesis 2 supplies a concrete basis for culmination: God has declared the man's aloneness, creatures have been presented and named, no corresponding helper has been found, and the woman is finally brought to him. Several hours can provide meaningful anticipation. The text does not state that several hours are insufficient.

### 8.2 Meaningful naming requires prolonged study

Campbell's strongest objection is that meaningful names reflecting animal nature or behavior would require extended observation, perhaps days per creature (Campbell 2014, citing Hughes 2004 and others).

This identifies the real disputed variable. Genesis says that Adam named the creatures and that his names stood. It does not state how long he deliberated or how semantically elaborate each name was.

The feasibility model gives 18–36 seconds per kind across its working range. God brings the creatures to Adam, eliminating search time. The categories are broad observational kinds rather than modern species-level taxonomic diagnoses. Form, movement, vocalization, posture, or another immediately perceptible characteristic could supply meaningful differentiation within seconds.

None of this proves that Adam used those cues. It shows that the text does not require the multi-day-per-creature assumption. The old-earth objection therefore depends on assigning a large value to a parameter Scripture leaves unstated.

At two days per thousand kinds, the naming alone would occupy about 5.5 years; at three days per kind, more than eight years. Such values are possible only by supplying chronology from outside the text, just as the five-hour model supplies a shorter value. The question is which values are required or excluded by the text. Genesis specifies neither.

### 8.3 Adam needed superhuman capacities

The model makes no appeal to superhuman cognition, perfect memory, extraordinary speech speed, or pre-Fall genius. Its claim is deliberately weaker: observing and assigning a label to a presented category within tens of seconds lies within ordinary human capability.

### 8.4 Clock orientation distorts the narrative

Following Poythress, Campbell argues that the narrative's orientation is relational and interactive rather than clock-driven (Poythress 2006, pp. 138–143, as cited in Campbell 2014). That literary observation is compatible with the present argument. The clock is an analytical instrument used to test a claim of temporal impossibility. It is not proposed as the narrative's theological focus.

### 8.5 Did the animals come in pairs?

Genesis 2 does not state that the animals approached Adam in pairs. Pairing may be narratively evocative because it highlights Adam's lack of a corresponding helper, but it remains an **illustrative reconstruction**. The timing model neither assumes nor requires it.

## 9. Implication for Pre-Fall History

Once the chronological objection is separated from the text, a larger canonical observation becomes visible. Day 6 establishes humanity's vocation and commission. The man receives work and a moral boundary; the woman is given as his corresponding partner; together they receive the command to be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, subdue it, and exercise dominion.

These commands describe activities whose execution follows their issuance. Filling, cultivation, reproduction, and stewardship require subsequent history. Genesis 1–2 does not specify how much time elapsed between the commissioning and the Fall.

Within the Biblical WorldModel architecture, Day 6 can therefore function as the **inauguration boundary of the human office**, while Pre-Fall History (PFH) investigates the interval in which that office could have been exercised before Genesis 3. This observation does not itself establish an extended pre-Fall chronology. It establishes a legitimate historical interval whose duration remains an open variable.

## 10. Conclusion

The sixth-day objection does not establish a chronological contradiction. Genesis 2 can be modeled coherently within an ordinary day without invoking superhuman naming ability, visionary presentation, or a highly compressed taxonomy.

The key distinctions are straightforward. The text gives the sequence and named categories. Grammar and canonical comparison supply defensible chronological inferences. Kind counts and hours are model parameters. Pairing and detailed staging belong to reconstruction.

A working range of 500–1,000 kinds fits comfortably within a five-hour naming interval at 18–36 seconds per kind. More importantly, even the deliberately overinclusive figure of 1,398 remains arithmetically feasible. Disputes over dinosaur baramins or exact created-kind counts therefore do not control the argument.

The remaining disagreement is narrower: how much deliberation did naming require? Scripture does not say. A claim that the process required days or years per group bears the same burden of justification as any shorter reconstruction.

The argument from **impossibility** therefore fails. What remains is interpretation of an unstated parameter.
