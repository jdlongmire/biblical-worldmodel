# Do Fossil Footprints Falsify Flood Geology?

## A response to “Outpaced by the Evidence: How Fossil Footprints Shoot Flood Geology in the Foot”

**J. D. Longmire**  
**11 September 2026**

A recent article at *The Evidence Is Plain* argues that fossil footprints expose a fatal contradiction in Flood geology. The argument deserves a careful answer because it identifies a real constraint that any catastrophic model must explain.

The basic observation is straightforward. Fossil trackways require a substrate that an animal could traverse, impressions that survived long enough to remain recognizable, and subsequent burial sufficient to preserve them. A model that required uninterrupted, high-energy erosion at the same location throughout the entire Flood could not easily account for such surfaces.

That is a legitimate criticism of such a model.

It does not, however, establish that every global-Flood model requires those conditions, that fossil footprints themselves require long geological ages, or that the conventional deep-time reconstruction follows uniquely from the evidence.

Those are separate propositions.

## What the footprints actually establish

A trace fossil directly establishes a limited historical sequence:

1. a mechanically traversable substrate existed;
2. an organism crossed it;
3. the resulting impressions survived subsequent disturbance;
4. later processes buried and lithified the surface.

The footprint itself does not contain a clock telling us whether the relevant interval lasted minutes, days, years, or much longer. Duration has to be established by additional evidence.

This distinction matters because a locally stable surface does not entail a regionally stable geological regime. Modern catastrophes are spatially and temporally heterogeneous. High-energy erosion in one location can occur contemporaneously with deposition, temporary exposure, ponding, drainage, or relative quiescence elsewhere. The same location can also move through different energy regimes as an event develops.

Accordingly, the relevant question for the Catastrophic Hydrotectonic Flood Model (CHFM) is more precise:

> Can CHFM generate a mechanically stable track-bearing substrate, allow sufficient time for animal passage, and then produce preservation-compatible burial from its own event dynamics?

If it cannot, the footprints are adverse evidence against CHFM. Merely pointing out that a quiet interval is logically possible would not be enough.

## The evidence is more interesting than the headline

Several well-known track sites illustrate why the broad claim is too strong.

At the St. George Dinosaur Discovery Site in Utah, track-bearing surfaces preserve mud cracks, ripple structures, scours, exposed mudflat conditions, shallow-water conditions, and repeated track-making. Utah Geological Survey descriptions also identify rapid sediment emplacement capable of preserving fine details of underlying surfaces. The evidence therefore records environmental oscillation and rapid burial. That is entirely consistent with the physical possibility of short-lived exposure and depositional pulses.

The Paluxy River track sites in the Glen Rose Formation of Texas likewise record dinosaurs traversing soft carbonate mud followed by burial. Multiple track layers and extensive invertebrate burrowing deserve further investigation because biological colonization may eventually supply useful minimum-duration constraints. Track preservation itself does not establish those durations.

Dinosaur Ridge in Colorado presents the stronger case.

Noffke, Hagadorn and Bartlett (2019) documented microbially induced sedimentary structures associated with dinosaur trackways, including multiple generations of microbial growth, disturbance, erosion, healing, and renewed track-making. Noffke (2026) interprets the sequence as at least three track-making events separated by approximately a year or more.

If that minimum duration is independently secure, it is significant evidence. CHFM should not evade it by simply asserting that microbial mats grew faster in the past. Such an auxiliary hypothesis would require empirical support.

But another question comes first.

## Chronology testing follows phase assignment

The Biblical WorldModel being evaluated here is not a simple “everything geological happened during one Flood year” chronology.

Its working historical architecture contains multiple stages:

**initialization and normalization → approximately 50,000–60,000 years of pre-Fall history → Fall → post-Fall/pre-Flood history → catastrophic Flood → post-Flood stabilization → approximately 5,000–6,000 years of written human history.**

That matters greatly.

A geological surface demonstrating several years of actual elapsed time would falsify a model only if that model requires the surface to have formed inside an interval shorter than those several years.

Therefore, before Dinosaur Ridge can falsify CHFM's Flood chronology, two propositions must be established:

1. the microbial structures independently require the proposed multi-year duration; and
2. the relevant Dinosaur Ridge horizon independently belongs within the CHFM Flood interval.

If the second proposition is not established, a multi-year surface may instead constrain where CHFM places the Flood boundary in the stratigraphic record. It does not falsify the historical architecture as a whole.

This is not permission to relocate every difficult geological horizon outside the Flood. That would make the model immunized against evidence. Phase assignments must themselves be constrained by stratigraphy, sedimentology, paleontology, geochemistry, structural relationships, and the model's prior commitments. A pattern of reactive reassignment would count against CHFM.

## Retrodiction and elapsed history

There is a broader methodological issue here.

Operational regularity warrants defeasible extrapolation. It does not entail that the system's initial state was generated by indefinitely extrapolating those same regularities backward.

Likewise, observing a physical state that is consistent with a long conventional history does not by itself demonstrate that the conventional historical trajectory occurred. The question is whether the evidence uniquely or strongly discriminates among competing histories.

For Dinosaur Ridge, microbial growth stages may eventually provide exactly such a discriminator. That is why the site is worth investigating rather than dismissing.

But “microbial structures are conventionally interpreted as representing years” and “the physical evidence independently requires years” are different claims. The latter is the one relevant to falsification.

## The asymmetry in the original argument

There is another methodological problem with the blogger's argument.

The article offers fossil footprints as evidence against Flood geology, but it does not state falsification criteria for the conventional historical reconstruction it favors.

That omission does not invalidate an internal critique of Flood geology. One can identify a contradiction in model A without first constructing model B.

The evidential situation changes when the conclusion becomes comparative: *therefore conventional deep-time geology is confirmed or should be preferred*.

At that point both historical reconstructions should expose their assumptions and failure conditions.

CHFM can state some of its own plainly. A track-bearing horizon would constitute serious adverse evidence if independent physical evidence demonstrated that it formed during the Flood interval yet required more elapsed time than CHFM permits. Likewise, repeated ecological succession, soil development, chemical weathering, microbial growth, or biological colonization with independently defensible minimum durations could falsify particular CHFM stratigraphic assignments.

The conventional reconstruction should face corresponding questions. What observation at these horizons would count against the long-duration interpretation? Which durations are independently constrained, and which are derived from actualistic process assumptions? What evidence would materially increase the probability of rapid catastrophic deposition? Are there observations that the conventional framework could not accommodate simply by changing local rates, hiatuses, depositional environments, or preservation conditions?

Without answers to questions like these, the comparison is asymmetric. One model is being asked to risk failure while the other functions as the interpretive background against which failure is judged.

## Where the evidence currently stands

The responsible conclusion is narrower than either polemic would suggest.

Fossil footprints do not intrinsically falsify catastrophic Flood geology. Sites such as St. George demonstrate that track formation, environmental oscillation, and rapid burial can coexist.

Some track-bearing horizons may nevertheless present serious problems for particular Flood chronologies. Dinosaur Ridge is currently a strong candidate because the microbial-mat succession may contain information about actual elapsed time rather than merely environmental state.

That question should be investigated directly.

For CHFM, the research programme therefore has two linked tasks: determine whether the Dinosaur Ridge microbial structures carry an independently defensible minimum duration, and determine where that horizon belongs within CHFM's multi-stage historical architecture.

If the evidence contradicts the model, the contradiction should remain on the ledger.

If the evidence proves compatible with more than one historical reconstruction, it should not be advertised as uniquely establishing any of them.

That is the standard I want applied to CHFM. It is also the standard I would ask its critics to apply to their own reconstruction.
