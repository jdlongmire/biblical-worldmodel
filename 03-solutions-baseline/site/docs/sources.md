# Sources and glossary

## Source discipline

Prefer primary sources. Distinguish observation, interpretation, hypothesis, and demonstrated result. Cite the source that actually supports a claim, and preserve its limits rather than rounding it up to a stronger one.

When only a secondary source is available, say so, and attribute the characterization to the secondary source rather than presenting it as the primary author's own words.

Programme repositories are listed in the [research guide](research-programmes.md). They retain authority over technical claims; this site translates and routes them for readers rather than restating them as its own findings.

## Working glossary

**Auxiliary hypothesis:** a supporting proposal that can be revised or rejected without silently changing every foundational commitment of the programme it belongs to. Part of a programme's protective belt.

**Burden of proof:** the obligation to supply supporting argument and evidence for a claim. A fair comparison places this obligation symmetrically; applying it only to one side of a debate, or only to the side one already disagrees with, is a comparison failure, not a neutral default.

**Canonical programme source:** the repository that owns a programme's technical claims and current research status. Where this site and a canonical source appear to disagree, the source controls.

**Falsifier:** a result that contradicts a specified claim under its stated testing conditions. Whether a particular result actually meets those conditions must be argued, not assumed by either side.

**Hard core:** the central commitments of a research programme, in the Lakatosian sense, that its adherents treat as not open to revision without abandoning the programme itself.

**Historical reconstruction:** a proposed account of past events connecting a model to present observations. See [methodology](methodology.md) for how this site tries to keep reconstruction distinct from observation.

**Inference:** a conclusion drawn from evidence using stated reasoning and assumptions. Naming the assumptions is part of the inference, not an optional footnote to it.

**Methodological naturalism:** the working discipline, common in the natural sciences, of explaining a phenomenon under investigation by appeal to natural causes and testable mechanisms. As a method it makes no claim about what does or does not exist beyond nature; that further claim is philosophical naturalism.

**Observation:** a recorded feature or measurement of the world, in principle checkable by repetition or independent instrument.

**Philosophical naturalism:** the metaphysical claim that the natural world is all that exists. This is a worldview commitment, not a finding produced by any particular measurement or method, and it is not entailed by methodological naturalism alone.

**Presupposition:** a foundational commitment that shapes what counts as evidence or a good explanation before a particular argument begins. Presuppositions can be examined, compared, and argued for or against, even though no line of argument starts from a position outside every worldview.

**Primary source:** a document, dataset, or artifact that directly records an observation, argument, or event, rather than reporting on one already recorded elsewhere.

**Progressive / degenerating programme:** Imre Lakatos's distinction between a research programme whose successive revisions predict genuinely new facts (progressive) and one whose revisions only accommodate results already known at the time of the revision (degenerating).

**Protective belt:** the auxiliary hypotheses surrounding a programme's hard core. These absorb the pressure of anomalous results and can be revised, in principle, without abandoning the core itself.

**Secondary source:** a document that reports, summarizes, or interprets a primary source rather than recording the original observation, measurement, or argument.

**Severe test:** a test capable of discriminating between competing explanations because a specified, publicly stated outcome would count against the hypothesis under test, not merely be reinterpreted to fit it after the fact.

**Worldview:** the set of foundational commitments through which a person interprets evidence, arguments, and experience. A worldview is not a conclusion reached free of commitments; it is the framework within which conclusions are reached, and it can itself be examined and compared against alternatives. See [compare frameworks](compare.md).

## Cited works

General epistemology and methodology terms used across this site draw on the following, cited here rather than repeated inline on every page that uses them:

- Bayes, T. (1763). "An Essay towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, 53, 370–418. Communicated posthumously by Richard Price.
- Popper, K. (1959). *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*. Hutchinson & Co. Expanded English translation of *Logik der Forschung* (1934/1935).
- Lakatos, I. (1970). "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes." In I. Lakatos & A. Musgrave (Eds.), *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge* (pp. 91–196). Cambridge University Press.

Sources specific to a research programme's own technical claims belong in that programme's own repository, listed at [research programmes](research-programmes.md), and are not duplicated here.
